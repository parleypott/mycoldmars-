sound research / burma / october 2026

the voice, alone

Ten recordings of Burmese voices with nothing — or almost nothing — underneath them. Plus a dissection of how Pat Metheny built "Above the Treetops" around three women from the Cambodian royal palace, so the moves are stealable.

part one / the reference

How "Above the Treetops" actually works

The voices that open Secret Story are not a field recording Metheny made. They're a lift from "Buong Suong," a royal invocation prayer — a song "in honour of the gods and spirits, to ensure the wellbeing of the king and the people," at least five or six generations old. It's track one of a 1971 UNESCO LP called Royal Music of Cambodia, recorded by French ethnomusicologist Jacques Brunet with the palace's permission. Metheny opened his album with the track that opened that album. Brunet is quietly credited on Secret Story as "Cambodian Ensembles."

In 1992 a journalist in Phnom Penh played the Metheny track for Cambodia's greatest living classical singer, Madame Em Theay. Her response: "That is me singing. There were three of us altogether." She'd joined the palace choir at age six. The other two voices on the sample — her teacher and her pupil — were both executed under the Khmer Rouge. Em Theay survived by claiming she'd been thrown out of the choir and sold vegetables. Metheny, by his own admission, "had heard it at some point and decided to use it, but had no idea of its origin or meaning."

The emptiness is what makes the voices feel sacred rather than produced.

What's under the voices on track 1: less than you think. The sources credit Charlie Haden's acoustic bass, Naná Vasconcelos and Armando Marçal on hand percussion, Mark Ledford and David Blamires singing wordless pads, and Metheny's own guitar-synth and keyboards. The famous London Orchestra sessions (Jeremy Lubbock, Abbey Road) are credited on tracks 2–14 — the lush bed under the choir on track 1 is almost certainly Metheny's own synth strings, not the orchestra. He built the whole album alone on a Synclavier first, then replaced synthetic parts with humans. 2:43 long: an invocation, not a song.

Two details worth checking against the physical booklet before quoting anywhere: the exact long-form choir credit, and the orchestra's absence from track 1 (strongly implied by the credits, not stated outright).

Hear the raw source

The actual 1971 LP streams free. Track 1 is Buong Suong — the unsampled, full ritual. This is the texture you're hunting in Burma.

The eight moves

  • 01 / open cold on the raw voiceNo fade-in, no harmonic preparation. The unaccompanied voice is the first sound on a 76-minute record. It declares the world before any instrument arrives.
  • 02 / build the bed under the voice, never the reverseThe sample is the immovable melodic truth. Transcribe its pitches first, then write pads to them. He chose harmony after and beneath the fixed vocal.
  • 03 / go modal and drone, not functionalNon-Western voices won't sit on equal-tempered moving changes. A sustained near-drone forgives microtonal drift and pitch-center wobble. Pick one or two anchor tones and float.
  • 04 / keep the bed sparse and darkAcoustic bass, hand percussion, soft pads — nearly it. Wordless background vocals double the choir's register instead of competing with it. Resist filling.
  • 05 / prototype synthetic, replace with humansHis Synclavier-first method: compose the whole arrangement in the box, then swap synth parts for real players and real rooms so the final bed breathes instead of looping.
  • 06 / reverb is the templeGive the dry, old, mono-ish source a believable physical room so the archival voice and your new elements share one space. The track lives on air and decay.
  • 07 / stay short and ceremonialUnder three minutes, as an overture. A fragile archival voice carries more weight as an invocation than stretched into a full song form.
  • 08 / know the provenanceMetheny got publicly caught not knowing what he'd sampled — and the real story (a royal prayer; two of three singers murdered) is heavier than the track. Trace the source, name the singers, understand the ritual. The ethics and the emotional payload are the same move.

part two / the board

Ten Burmese voices

Ordered from purest to most accompanied. Green badge = genuinely nothing under the voice. Anything flagged, your sampler can strip.

01

Pali Paritta: 11 Suttas — Ven. Sayadaw U Silananda

solo monk · burmese theravada chant · 13 tracks
pure voicedownloadable stems

Low, steady, hypnotic male recitation — non-metrical prayer cadence over total silence. The Ratanasutta runs long and settles into a rhythmic drone; best single pull on the whole board. Every track downloads as clean MP3/OGG.

02

Mingun Sayadaw — Paritta Gyi, 11 Suttas

solo monk · historic recording · aged tape
pure voicearchival texture

The famous memory-feat monk of the Sixth Buddhist Council (he recited the entire Pali canon from memory). Weathered tape hiss, a once-in-a-century timbre. The crackle is itself an instrument.

03

Pa Auk Sayadaw — Jayamangala Gatha

solo monk · melodic sung chant
pure voice

The most sung of the chants — a rolling melodic cadence rather than flat recitation. Of all the monk material, this is the one closest to the Cambodian-choir feeling of melody suspended over nothing.

04

"Mommy It's Sleepy Time" — Truetender Htun

solo female · burmese lullaby · vermont folklife field recording
pure voiceintimate / domestic

A bare human melody rather than chant — female, close-mic'd, sung the way it would be sung to a child. The strongest contrast texture to the monks, and the most intimate thing on the board.

05

Myanmar In Sound — field recordings, Bagan & Mandalay

group + solo chant in the wild · 2015 ethnography
voice + ambienceunstaged

Track 3: a schoolyard of children chanting, then a solo temple voice. Track 11: rooftop chanting over the city. Raw, un-enhanced, with the world baked in — the closest thing here to the group-voice texture Metheny sampled.

06

Kachin Manau Welcome Chant — "Manau poi kawn hkap tau la"

kachin / jinghpaw · group call-and-response · festival invocation
expect drums + gong under voices

Group antiphony — a leader calls, the crowd answers in "oh ra ra" refrains. The one true choir texture on the board. Manau chant traditionally rides gong and drum, so plan to carve.

07

Lahpai La Ja — Mahkawn Dingsa (Kachin Old Song)

kachin / jinghpaw · solo old-style song
accompaniment unconfirmed — likely light

Old-style solo Jinghpaw song — a lament register you don't get from the Buddhist material. Worth an ear for a lone mountain voice.

08

Akha Song — "Ya Sm Ga Ma"

akha · hill-tribe village song · eastern shan state
verify by ear — village song is often voice-forward

"When we used to walk to the fields together." Akha village singing from the hills where Shan State meets the Golden Triangle — plainspoken, worn, nothing performed about it.

09

San Toke — "Burma: Shan Song"

shan · solo male voice + single reed · recorded 1951
one reed instrument under the voice

Lonesome, modal, with seven decades of archival hiss — reads "old world" instantly. Voice plus one hne-type reed trading the melody; the reed strips easily.

listen at smithsonian folkways →

also on spotify / apple music — search "San Toke Burma Shan Song"

10

Mahagitá — Inle Myint Maung & Yi Yi Thant

burmese classical song corpus · voice + saung harp · rangoon 2000
harp present — this is the one to strip

The Burmese classical canon sung by one of its most respected voices. Harp and voice trade the same melody, so the voice sits nearly alone in long passages. The "beautiful and bare" end of the court tradition — your sampler's first job.

part three / if the board isn't enough

Deeper wells

  • Pa Auk — 35-track paritta archive — 444MB of downloadable solo monk chant. The biggest clean-stem well found.
  • Mingun Sayadaw — Metta Sutta — the loving-kindness text; gentler cadence than the protective suttas.
  • Victoria Vorreiter — Songs of Memory — unaccompanied Akha, Lahu, Lisu and Karen village vocals. The single best match for pure sparse hill-tribe voice; her albums are purchase-only, the channel has excerpts.
  • PARADISEC Kachin collections — ~2,700 Jinghpaw field recordings including sung mahkawn. Gated behind free registration; gold if you want raw solo ethnic voice at scale.
  • The Crying Princess — 78rpm Records from Burma — 1909–1960 shellac, voice-led over period harp and piano. Previews only, but the crackle-and-ache register is unmatched. Start with the title track.
  • Burmese Folk & Traditional Music (Folkways, 1953) — ensemble-heavy, but track 2 "Byaw Than" (novitiation ceremonial song) is a strong voice-forward ceremonial.
