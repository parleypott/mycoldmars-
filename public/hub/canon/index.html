DOSSIER Nº 00119.7°N 102.0°W

The Town That Fired Everyone

Cherán K'eri, Michoacán, Mexico · resilience

The human anchor: José Trinidad Ramírez Tapia, member of the first Concejo Mayor, and the women of the fogatas who blocked the logging trucks at dawn on April 15, 2011

Before sunrise, women climb to El Calvario, the highest point in town, and stop the logging trucks with their bodies. That was fifteen years ago. Today Cherán has no political parties, no mayor, no police — and it works. The barricade bonfires, the fogatas, never went out: 189 of them became the town's parliament, kitchen, and classroom, where neighbors elect their council face to face. A volunteer patrol called the ronda walks the streets at night, unpaid, as their ancestors did. The forest the criminals stripped — up to 20,000 hectares — has been replanted past 100%, schoolchildren carrying the seedlings. On a plaque, 17 names of the dead, and a promise: their names flower in every tree growing in Cherán's sky.

The bigger picture: Across Mexico, organized crime and party politics have hollowed out local government. Cherán is the country's living counterfactual: the first community whose Supreme Court-recognized indigenous self-rule replaced both — and delivered lower violence and a resurrected forest. It makes the abstract question 'what is a state actually for?' concrete in one small mountain town.

DOSSIER Nº 00224.4°N 118.3°E

The Island That Forges China's Bombs Into Kitchen Knives

Maestro Wu (Kin Ho Li) forge, Jinning Township, Kinmen, Taiwan · resilience

The human anchor: Wu Tseng-dong (吳增棟), third-generation bladesmith of the Kin Ho Li knife factory, founded 1937

A customer walks into a forge on Kinmen and points at a rusted artillery shell on the stack. Wu Tseng-dong cuts it open with a torch, heats a slab of its steel to orange, and hammers out a cleaver while they wait. The shell was fired at his island to destroy it. In 1958 China dropped over 470,000 rounds on Kinmen in 44 days, then kept shelling for two more decades, the explosives eventually swapped for propaganda leaflets. Wu's grandfather started collecting the casings; soldiers brought shells in and asked for knives to carry home. One shell yields dozens of blades. Sixty-plus years on, the bombardment meant to erase this island is still being carried out of the shop, one kitchen knife at a time.

The bigger picture: The Taiwan Strait standoff is usually told as maps, missiles, and hypotheticals. On Kinmen, a frozen front line a few kilometers from Xiamen's skyline, the conflict is literal raw material: a family business that has metabolized the entire artillery war into cutlery. As invasion talk returns, the knives are both souvenir and warning.

DOSSIER Nº 00334.6°N 11.2°E

The Crab They Named After ISIS Made Them Rich

Kerkennah Islands, off Sfax, Tunisia · absurd

The human anchor: Sassi Alaya, president of the Al Ganoush fishing development group representing 600+ Kerkennah fishermen; fishermen like Mouradh who watched the invasion arrive, and the fishermen's wives who invented the recipes that sold the crab to Tunisia

Dawn off Kerkennah, and the nets come up shredded. Around 2014 an American blue crab rode warming water through Suez and started eating everything: bream, mullet, octopus, the nets themselves. The fishermen gave it the worst name they knew: Daech. ISIS. For three years it was a plague with claws. Then somebody noticed Asia pays for this animal. A factory opened on the islands in 2019. Wives of fishermen learned to cook it, ran public tastings to convince a country that doesn't eat crab. By 2021 the monster was 25% of Tunisia's fish exports, $24 million, 48 processing plants. Now Sassi Alaya scans the shallows with a new fear, the strangest one of all: what if Daech runs out?

The bigger picture: The Mediterranean is one of the fastest-warming seas on Earth, and the Suez Canal has become a one-way conveyor of invasive species remaking its ecosystems. Kerkennah is the rare place where a fishing community absorbed the blow and reversed it, which raises the harder question every warming coastline will face: can adaptation outrun invasion, and what happens when your economy starts depending on the catastrophe?

DOSSIER Nº 0049.4°N 75.8°W

The Donkey Wears Couture

San Antero, Córdoba, Colombia · absurd

The human anchor: Andrés Álvarez, winner of the 2026 donkey costume contest with 'Efectos del Cambio Climático', and the costume-makers of San Antero

Every Holy Week since 1925, this Caribbean fishing town does something gloriously strange: it dresses its donkeys. Not with a hat — with full theatrical productions. The 2026 champion, built by Andrés Álvarez, turned a donkey into a rolling diorama of the February floods: drowning animals, a man in a boat carrying everything he owns, furniture floating past. It won 10 million pesos and it made people laugh and cry at once. The week starts with a Judas effigy paraded on donkey-back and burned, and swerves through a 4 a.m. musical wake-up, rice-pounding contests, crab-eating contests, and a donkey-imitation championship for humans. The donkey — the unglamorous animal that built this region — gets one week a year as king.

The bigger picture: The festival is folk media: a town processing its year — floods, poverty, church, politics — through satire stitched onto a donkey. It's also a case study in how rural Latin American traditions survive by mutating, turning a 1925 religious procession into a national event that carries climate grief inside a joke. Comedy in its bones, exactly the register the season needs.

DOSSIER Nº 0059.6°N 44.1°E

The Book Fair of a Country That Doesn't Exist

Hargeysa Cultural Centre, Hargeisa, Somaliland (unrecognized), Somalia de jure · joy

The human anchor: Jama Musse Jama, the mathematician who founded the Hargeysa International Book Fair in 2008 and still directs it; patron Edna Adan Ismail; the young volunteers and readers who fill the Cultural Centre every July

Every July, in the capital of a country no government on Earth recognizes, more than 10,000 people queue for books. Hargeisa was flattened by war in 1988; Jama Musse Jama, a mathematician who came home, started a book fair in 2008 with 200 people and a conviction that a nation is made of stories before it is made of treaties. Eighteen editions later there are poetry workshops, book launches, Somali song and dance, delegations from 13 countries, a guest nation each year (Zambia in 2025), and a Moving Library that tours the regions before the fair. Diplomats won't recognize Somaliland. Writers do. For six days a year, the passport nobody stamps doesn't matter; the poem does.

The bigger picture: Somaliland has functioned as a self-governing democracy for three decades without a single country recognizing it, a limbo that locks it out of banks, aid, and treaties. The book fair is statecraft by other means: soft power, cultural diplomacy, and nation-building run on poetry instead of recognition. It makes concrete an abstract question, what actually makes a country real, the map or the people who believe in it?

DOSSIER Nº 00659.9°N 8.6°E

The Town That Built Its Own Sun

Rjukan, Telemark, Norway · joy

The human anchor: Martin Andersen, the artist who moved to Rjukan and spent twelve years getting three mirrors bolted to the mountainside

Rjukan sits so deep in an east-west valley that the sun abandons it from late September to mid-March. Not Arctic darkness — crueler than that: you can see sunlight on the mountaintops, golden and near, and never touch it. In 1913 a bookkeeper named Oscar Kittelsen proposed mirrors on the north wall to pour light down on the pale factory workers. The idea sat for a hundred years. Then Martin Andersen, an artist pushing a stroller through the shadow, decided to finish it. Twelve years of persuasion later, three computer-steered mirrors, 450 meters up, track the sun and throw a 600-square-meter ellipse of daylight onto the town square. In October 2013 the clouds parted and hundreds of people stood in borrowed sunshine, faces up, blinking.

The bigger picture: What a community owes its own well-being, and how long an idea can wait for its technology. Rjukan is a company town built by industrial giants who moved mountains of water for power but couldn't give workers the sun — the mirror closes a century-old promise. It's also the gentlest possible story about engineering as an act of love for a place.

“This Norwegian Town Didn't See the Sun for 5 Months. So They Built One.”

DOSSIER Nº 00721.0°N 105.8°E

The Cafe Street the Government Cannot Kill

Train Street (Tran Phu–Phung Hung segment), Hanoi Old Quarter, Vietnam · absurd

The human anchor: The cafe-owning families living in the row houses along the 300-meter rail corridor spanning Dien Bien Phu, Cua Nam, and Hoan Kiem wards

Twice a day, a horn sounds down a Hanoi alley and a whole neighborhood folds itself up. Tables slide in, stools stack, tourists press against walls, and a full-size train shaves past the espresso machines with a meter to spare. Then the street blooms back out. Families have lived on this corridor for generations; the cafes are their living rooms turned inside out. The city has 'closed' the street repeatedly. Owners kill the lights when police pass and pour coffee when they're gone; two days after the November 2025 clearance it was packed again. Now Hanoi has a new idea: instead of removing the cafes, reroute the trains themselves, which would delete the exact danger everyone comes to feel.

The bigger picture: Every fast-growing tourist city faces this equation: the thing visitors love is often informal, unsafe, and technically illegal, and the livelihoods it feeds are real. Train Street is over-tourism, state control, and family economics compressed into 300 meters, where the state may finally win by moving the trains and killing the magic.

DOSSIER Nº 00816.4°S 68.1°W

The World's Highest Ski Resort Has No Snow

Chacaltaya, Cordillera Real, near La Paz, Bolivia · loss

The human anchor: Bernardo Guarachi, 68, veteran Bolivian mountaineer who skied Chacaltaya in its glory years and still walks its ruins; glaciologist Edson Ramírez of Universidad Mayor de San Andrés, who measured the glacier to its end

At 5,300 meters, a rusting ski lift climbs a mountain of bare rock. The Club Andino Boliviano built this in the 1940s — the highest ski run on Earth, an Alpine-style lodge above the clouds, a truck engine dragging skiers up a tropical glacier 30 kilometers from La Paz. Bolivia trained Olympic skiers here. South America held its first championship here. Then scientists arrived in the 1990s and gave the ice until 2015. It didn't make it: in August 2009, Chacaltaya became the first tropical glacier in the modern era to melt away completely. Bernardo Guarachi, 68, walks up alone among the cables and calls it a cemetery. But the lodge still stands, the club still exists, and the mountain still teaches — as the warning La Paz's two million people can see from their windows.

The bigger picture: Chacaltaya is the visible edge of the Andean water crisis: the tropical glaciers feeding La Paz and El Alto are following it downhill, and glaciologists say every similar glacier is on the same curve. One dead ski lift makes a hemispheric story — climate change as something a city can watch happen from its own streets — while the ski club's stubborn memory keeps it from being despair.

DOSSIER Nº 00929.6°N 106.6°E

The Last Bang Bang Men of the Stair City

Jiefangbei and Chaotianmen, Chongqing, China · loss

The human anchor: The last few thousand bang bang porters of central Chongqing, and filmmaker He Ku, who apprenticed under porter Lao Huang (Huang Zeqing, d. 2021) for the documentary 'The Last Bang Bang'

Chongqing is a city built on cliffs, where the street outside a 10th-floor window can be another street. For decades its cargo moved on the shoulders of the bang bang army: men with a bamboo pole and two ropes, waiting on corners for someone to shout for a carry. At the peak there were 300,000 of them. Now, maybe a few thousand, mostly old men, undercut by escalators, elevators, and delivery apps. In 2014 a filmmaker named He Ku moved into a condemned alley with 1,300 yuan and apprenticed under a 65-year-old porter called Lao Huang, who had raised a daughter alone on his pole. The film he made became a national mirror. Lao Huang died in 2021. The last porters still wait on the corners.

The bigger picture: China's economic miracle was carried, literally, by its first generation of rural migrant workers, and that generation is now aging out with thin pensions and vanishing trades. The bang bang men are the miracle's human ledger: what a country owes the bodies that built it, asked in the world's most vertical city.

DOSSIER Nº 01019.0°N 72.8°E

The City That Lost Its Vultures

Doongerwadi, Malabar Hill, Mumbai, India · loss

The human anchor: Mumbai's Parsi Zoroastrian community — Dhan Baria, who forced the crisis into the open in 2005 with photographs of her mother's undecayed body, and Khojeste Mistree of the Bombay Parsi Punchayet, defending the tradition's future.

In the middle of Mumbai, on the city's most expensive hill, sits a forest almost no one may enter. Inside stand the Towers of Silence, where for centuries Parsis have laid their dead under the open sky, to be returned to nature by vultures within hours. Then the vultures vanished — 99% of them, poisoned by diclofenac, a cheap painkiller given to the cattle they also ate. The ritual's engine was gone. Now solar concentrators focus heat onto the towers; they fail at night and in monsoon. Dhan Baria broke the taboo and showed the community what that means. Traditionalists want an aviary and the birds back; conservationists have been breeding vultures since 2004, holding the species — and maybe the ritual — in trust.

The bigger picture: One veterinary drug, approved in the early 1990s, collapsed an entire ecological guild across South Asia in a decade — one of the fastest bird declines ever recorded — and its most intimate casualty is a 3,000-year-old funeral rite in the heart of a megacity. It is the clearest case on earth of how invisible chemical systems ripple into culture: pharmacology deciding theology. The thread out is real — captive-bred vultures now exist in numbers, and a community is negotiating, fiercely and openly, how to carry its dead until the sky comes back.

DOSSIER Nº 01149.0°N 123.1°W

The Town America Forgot to Attach

Point Roberts, Washington, United States · connection

The human anchor: Ali Hayton, who has run the exclave's only grocery store since 1998; Tamra Hansen, owner of the Saltwater Cafe; Brian Calder, 83, the town's unofficial advocate writing unanswered letters to the B.C. Premier

Point Roberts is thirteen square kilometers of America stapled to the bottom of a Canadian peninsula. Its water comes from Canada. Its electricity comes from Canada. Its children ride through Canada to reach American school. Ninety percent of its economy is Canadian friendship. Then a president talks about tariffs and a 51st state, and the friendship stops coming through the border gate. Ali Hayton watches her grocery store fall from 8,000 customers a week to under 2,000. Tamra Hansen's cafe drops forty percent. Brian Calder, 83, writes to the Premier of British Columbia asking for mercy for a town that never chose any of this. The letter goes unanswered. The town waits, kettle on, for its neighbors to come back.

The bigger picture: When superpowers fight trade wars, the casualties are drawn on the map at the seams. Point Roberts is the purest laboratory of interdependence on Earth: a piece of one country that physically cannot function without the goodwill of another. Every abstraction of the 2025-26 US-Canada rupture — tariffs, boycotts, nationalism — becomes concrete in one grocery store's weekly receipts.

THUMB: Aerial of the tiny peninsula with a hard red border line slicing across it, one small American flag below the line, everything above shaded Canadian red

DOSSIER Nº 01239.5°N 0.4°W

The Water Court That Meets Every Thursday on the Cathedral Steps

Puerta de los Apóstoles, Valencia Cathedral, Plaza de la Virgen, Valencia, Spain · connection

The human anchor: The eight síndics of the Tribunal de les Aigües — one per irrigation canal — who judge water disputes in the open air; among them José Alfonso Soria García, síndic of the Mislata acequia, who served as the court's president.

Every Thursday at noon, eight men in black smocks carry chairs to the Gothic door of Valencia's cathedral and hold court in the open air. No lawyers, no paper, no appeal. A farmer accused of stealing water from his neighbor's turn states his case aloud, in Valencian, and the síndics of the eight irrigation canals rule on the spot. This is the Tribunal de les Aigües, and it has met this way for roughly a thousand years, older than any parliament in Europe. The huerta it governs — an Andalusi web of acequias threading between the city's tower blocks — survives because these growers still trust neighbors to divide a scarce thing fairly, by voice, in daylight, in front of everyone.

The bigger picture: Long before Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel for showing communities can govern shared resources without markets or states, Valencia's farmers were doing it. As drought tightens across the Mediterranean and water becomes the century's contested resource, this thousand-year-old oral court is a living argument that the commons can be managed by trust and reputation rather than lawyers and meters — and that the fastest, cheapest justice can be the oldest.
