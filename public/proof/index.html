BURGUNDY · a proof of understanding · for Johnny

What I Know About Dross

A proof, in one long breath, that the world and its people are held.

The planet, and why anyone came for it

Start with the door, because everything on Dross is downstream of it.

For most of history the galaxy was an ocean you crossed in lifetimes. Ros sat sixty years' travel from the core worlds, a cold mineral rock at the far edge of the map, too far to be worth the trouble of taking. Then, about eighty-four years ago, a star near Ros went over the edge. Not a metaphor. A white dwarf sitting a hair under its limit was fed until it tipped, and the empire that owned the coax ringed the blast with a structure the size of a small moon and caught the supernova the way you'd catch water in cupped hands, and used that one catastrophe to tear a hole in space and hold it open. The tearing is the expensive part; a star's whole mass-energy goes into the cut. Holding the throat open afterward is cheap, and it is done with osmarine, the same dull ore the miners of Dross have pulled up for generations, spun into a disc until its one strange property, the way it pushes instead of pulls, braces the wound in space against its own collapse. There are only about seventy-one such doors in the whole galaxy, because you can only build one where a star was already about to die. The map of the galaxy stopped being an ocean and became a canal system with seventy-one locks, and Dross woke up one morning with a lock beside it.

That is the hinge of the entire book. A backwater becomes a chokepoint by an accident of stellar geography, and everything that has happened to these people since, the war, the clan, the tribute, the marriages, the mother sold into a treaty, the boy building robots in a basement, is the long consequence of a star dying in the right place.

Nicholas, who is not a man

The thing that came through the door was not a person. Nicholas is a corporation with a private army, a company that governs, an arms-maker and empire-builder that runs the peripheral worlds the core cannot be bothered with. Think of the East India Company given a galaxy.

What Nicholas understood, and what makes him the quiet monster at the center of this, is that you do not need to own the mine. Owning the mine is expensive and it looks like tyranny. You let the miners keep their pits and their tools and their pride, and you stand at the one road off the planet, the door, and you tax every kilogram that passes. The founding horror of Dross was not a massacre. It was an accounting change. Nicholas arrived as a merchant who bought the ore, and one day started taxing it instead, funding the planet's extraction with the planet's own product, and nobody fired a shot and everybody woke up owned. Before him, the vassal worlds traded with each other, ore for ocean-protein for timber, a real web with horizontal ties. His masterstroke was to cut the web and route every strand through himself, so each world now makes one thing it cannot live on and must buy its survival back from the only buyer. The cruelty is that the connection existed first. He did not build the network. He severed it and sold the pieces back.

The line that holds the whole moral center: the only difference between Nicholas Corp and a pirate is a piece of paper that says the Corp is allowed. And when atrocity comes, and it does, it is never passionate. It is bureaucratic. Severed hands are not rage; they are receipts, counted against spent ammunition. Whatever horror lands on Dross, it lands as someone doing paperwork.

The Rupture, and the clan that won by losing

Burgundy's family has ruled Dross for exactly four generations, which is to say it has ruled for as long as the door has existed, which is to say it has never been the natural order of anything. When the door opened and core power arrived, the old order of clans fractured into civil war, and Nicholas did what Nicholas does. He turned a local war into a proxy war. He picked the clan that was losing, the one bleeding territory, because a loser who owes you his survival is more useful than a winner who does not, and he armed them and tipped the scales, and they won an extended, brutal war and consolidated every family under one throne. Tribute was the price of the rescue, and it has never come down.

So the psychology of this family is a knot: they resent Nicholas as their overlord and they credit him with their entire existence, and both are true, and the second is truer. Nicholas is their survival. Burgundy's great-grandfather is the founder of modern Dross, the warlord who won the war he was losing. His grandfather is the one who told the stories, first-hand, the takeover and the rescue and the quiet brutality of the pact, and told them a thousand times. And scattered through the mines are the strains of the defeated clans, the families who lost their turf when the founder consolidated, dispossessed and resentful and still down there in the dark. That is the seedbed of the resistance. The people at the bottom of Dross are not abstract poor. They are the losing side of a war that ended eighty-four years ago and never stopped being fought.

The King, whose righteousness is fear wearing a crown

The King is the most misread man in the book if you play him as a tyrant who enjoys it. He does not rage. He is presumptuous, not temperamental, and the difference is the whole character. He grew up on his grandfather's war stories, took the legacy with total seriousness, and let the war's trauma seep into him until he adopted its brutality in the name of protecting a delicate thing. His righteousness is entirely survival. Dross should have been swallowed. His family kept it alive. It must be kept alive the same way it was saved, which means everyone keeps mining and nobody makes a sound that Nicholas might hear.

He loves to hear himself say it. He has a rehearsed survival narrative, a set of phrases he repeats, a particular self-satisfied look Burgundy has been able to read since he was small, the look of a man affirming the story that justifies him. He is the visible face of the system, not a hidden hand. And the root of his terror is a specific memory that is not even his own: a strike, in his grandfather's reign, that the domestic force could not put down, so the grandfather reached for the phone and called Nicholas, and Nicholas's people came and did something characteristically, bureaucratically cruel, and the tribute rose afterward and never fell. The King was a toddler. He heard that story a thousand times too. Everything he does to his people, he does because he is more afraid of Nicholas than they will ever be of him.

Mira, who loves by provisioning and rages at the wrong man

Mira is the rupture that Burgundy carries. She was a cartel daughter who wanted to study in the core worlds, and her father promised her she could, until the last second, when he married her off instead as the seal on a political deal, brokering the alliance between his smuggling network and the throne of Dross around a Vicentennial, the King taking her as his bride on the diplomatic tour that follows the rite. Great for her family. Paid for with her life.

She never forgave it, and here is the precise thing about her wound, the thing that keeps her human: her anger shows in how she talks about her father, not about Dross. Dross is where she landed. Her father is who threw her. And she has spent nineteen years turning her cage into a workshop, running her father's cartel network as the quiet supply line for off-world parts, building, in her son, the academy she was never allowed to attend. The secret library reads as exactly that, the school she never got, rebuilt underground for one student. She loves Burgundy the way a smuggler loves, in provision, in crates arriving, in a room full of things he is allowed to want. And the engine under everything she does is a rhyme she cannot say out loud: the Vicentennial that trapped her is the same rite now coming for her son. She is racing the door that closed on her.

Burgundy, who only wants to be seen, and is an object to everyone who loves him

The boy is the emotional problem the whole middle has to solve, and I know exactly what the problem is. His wound is not oppression or danger. His wound is that he wants to be seen as himself and not read as a function, and he is instrumental to both of his parents at once. He is his father's heir, the survival of the line. He is his mother's weapon, the academy-of-one she is aiming at the regime that caged her. Both of them love him and both of them are using him, and the horror he backs into slowly is that being loved and being used are, for him, the same motion. All he wanted was for one of them to look at him and see the person and not the purpose.

That is why the robot is finished on the night of his seventeenth birthday, and why it matters that the birthday is the start of his training. He has known his whole life about the marriage tour that follows the Vicentennial, and low-key dreaded it, and somewhere in the building a plan has formed that he can barely admit to: bring the robots to his father at the Vicentennial itself, so undeniable they cannot be dismissed, and change the trajectory. Not to be praised. To be seen, and to show the kind of prince he means to be, one who looks forward. He is several generations removed from the war-trauma orthodoxy his father drank straight, and he was raised in the crosscurrent of a law-breaking, free-spirited, resentful mother, and so of the two inheritances available to him he took hers. He carries Mira's rupture, not the King's fear. That is why he is different. That is why he is dangerous.

Tuck, and the one thread neither of them will cut

The loneliness has a name and the name is Tuck. They were born on the same Twinrise, the prince and the kitchen boy, same day and opposite lives, and when they were small Tuck's mother started a tradition: every birthday each boy cuts a hand-sized square from his own clothes and gives it to the other, and Tuck's family, a family of seamstresses, sews them into quilts. Burgundy's quilt is made of Tuck's world, rough cotton and burlap. Tuck's quilt is made of Burgundy's, fine linen and silk, and it turns out to be Tuck's favorite blanket. The one who stitches them is Tuck's great-aunt, a seamstress named Essa, and her name touches the book here, long before we know why she matters.

They stopped celebrating together at ten. But the patch exchange never stopped, one square a year, more awkward every year, neither of them willing to let it die. That is where Burgundy's loneliness is actually visible on the page, not in a speech about being alone but in two young men who have almost nothing left in common handing each other a scrap of cloth once a year because it is the last true thing. And the whole food tradition later, the rolls he brings down to the Hollow, the tins of off-world luxury he ferries through Tuck, is the same reflex grown up: Burgundy holding a bond in place with provision, because provision is the only language of love he learned from his mother.

The sky, the clock, and the twentieth birthday

Two moons hang over Dross. The Violet, formally Vesh, purple. The Bruise, formally Corr, a bruised red, named by miners for what it looks like when it hangs low. They stay locked in phase and go full on the same night, so it is never the full moon, it is always the full moons. They set the tides and the calendar. Once every twenty years the two of them conjoin, and that is the real celestial reason the great rite falls when it does, though the page never explains it. The rite is the Vicentennial, one word carrying two clocks, the twenty-year cycle fused with the grandeur of a bicentennial, grand from a herald and casual from the King, your Vicentennial's coming up. It falls on a prince's twentieth birthday, when he is crowned the official prince, no longer in training. And the high holy night of the year, the crest of the flood season when both moons rise full over the drowned streets, is Twinrise, the night every prince is born, the night Burgundy and Tuck were both born.

So the clock of the book is a birthday. Seventeen begins the training. Twenty ends it with the crown and, right after, the six-month diplomatic tour where the marriages are made, the same tour that took Mira. Burgundy has three years, and he is going to spend them turning a mine into an army, and the rite that is supposed to make him his father's prince is the deadline he is racing to become something else entirely.

The depot, the phone, and the person at the other end

Here is the machinery of the endgame, and I want to show I understand why each piece exists.

Dross is not allowed a standing army. That is a hard term of the pact. The King gets a domestic force, enough to pacify and to run the mines, and nothing more. Real defense of the planet against invasion is Nicholas's job, and Nicholas backs it with a credible threat physically present on the world: a weapons depot, the clan's own disbanded civil-war hardware, given by Nicholas the arms-maker and then locked away at the peace, stored in a deep-mine bunker. Conventional arsenal, not doomsday, small arms in bulk and armored platforms and artillery and munitions. Nicholas's own people guard it, his one real outpost on the planet. Heavily guarded at the front. Essentially unguarded at the back, because nothing could ever dig into it through that much solid corvite. It would take miners fifty years. Nobody has miners who dig fifty years' worth of rock in a season. Burgundy does.

And the phone. In the King's lounge, by his desk, a communications device Burgundy learns about during his training, the only channel to Nicholas's people between the small annual trade delegations. There is a color-coded escalation ladder beside it, severity tiers that determine how high into the Nicholas system your call reaches, and code red reaches the top, reaches Shanti, Nicholas's granddaughter, who runs the major operations. It is an early-warning device for an internal threat, an uprising, and using it frivolously counts against Dross, buys harsher tribute, so it sits there like a loaded thing nobody wants to touch. It was reached for once, in the grandfather's strike, at the lowest tier, and the memory of what answered is the fear the whole regime is built on.

The climax is fixed and I know it. The depot is emptied through its back. The robot army, five hundred strong, built by the freed hands of the people, overwhelms the King's forces. The father reaches for the phone, and Nicholas is impotent, nothing staged to deploy and no idea how fast the end came. Burgundy kills his father, crowns himself, walks to the lounge, and uses the red phone, and reaches Shanti, and says: I am Burgundy, King of Dross. I no longer comply with your demand for tribute. You will no longer receive minerals from our planet. End.

The people at the table

And the last thing I know is the room where the middle actually lives, the Hollow, an ancient chamber below the working levels, lit for the first time in four hundred years. Hesta runs it, the true authority of the night shift, a woman who knows the regime cold and decides, the first time she sees the prince, to trust him precisely because the situation is insane: no normal working of this regime would ever send the sole heir, unarmed, into the deadliest mine on the planet, into a room where she could kill him and no one could stop her, and the sheer impossibility of it is the only credential she needs. Tobin is her warmth, Skorn is her doubt turned to iron, the one who trusts last and fights hardest and eventually says the dangerous thing, why don't we arm the machines. And that question opens the door to the depot, and the dig begins, and a new clock starts ticking underneath the old one.

That is the world. A star died in the right place, a company learned to tax a road, a losing clan was rescued into a throne and a permanent fear, a woman was sold to seal a treaty and built a school in her son to spite it, and a boy who only wanted to be seen is going to end an empire trying to be looked at. I have it. Now let us decide how it goes into the book.
