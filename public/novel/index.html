Chapter One

The Basement

Burgundy had been awake for thirty-one hours.

He could tell by the count blinking in the corner of his monitor, the timer he set to motivate himself. The number didn't bother him. The shake in his right hand did.

It was long past midnight, which made the birthday over. His seventeenth. The feast had run late, the long tables in the great hall taking hours to empty, and he had come down here while the lamps in the upper corridors were still being put out.

In the morning he would walk into the Kings' Hall for the first council of his training. That was what seventeen meant, for a prince of Dross. Nobody had asked him and nobody had needed to: at seventeen a prince presents himself and begins to learn to rule, and for a week the palace had been readying him for that door, the council coat delivered to his rooms, the schedule copied out in a clerk's hand, the same quiet bustle it gave a collection day.

The robot under the tarp was his own idea. Nobody had asked for that either.

Tomorrow morning his father would look at him longer than he had in years. He wanted that look to land on the machine first.

Then him.

The basement smelled like smoking copper. Cables ran along the floor in bundles thicker than his arm, taped down with whatever he'd had on hand: duct tape, electrical tape, in one place a strip of his own shirt. Against the far wall, beneath a tarp, the prototype waited.

He hadn't looked at it in six hours. That was on purpose. If he looked he'd find something to fix, and he didn't have time to fix anything else. Tomorrow was the day.

Three monitors glowed in front of him: torque curve on the left, strain-gauge table in the middle, and on the right a text editor open on a function he had named GO. On the third line from the top there was an off-by-one error he had been looking at for almost an hour without seeing.

He saw it now. He pressed the tab key. He pressed it again. He pressed it a third time and committed the fix and ran the simulation again and watched the torque curve resolve into something less ugly than it had been.

He let out a breath. An eye twitched.

Above him, somewhere, the palace was quiet. The palace was always quiet at this hour. Even the kitchens, which started before dawn, had not yet started. Burgundy could hear the boiler in the next sublevel, a low irregular cough that had been part of the basement's soundtrack for so long that he no longer heard it unless he listened. He listened for a moment. Then he stopped listening and pressed his hands against his eyes and counted to ten.

He knew the kitchens better than a prince was supposed to. When he was small he had spent more hours there than anywhere in the palace, on a stool near the ovens where it was warm, watching Tuck's mother work.

Tuck's family had cooked for the crown for four generations. People said it the same way every time, four generations, as though it were a title, and in a way it was.

Tuck's mother was one of the few people his own mother trusted. She raised Burgundy in between her kitchen shift, and sometimes during, placing him on a stool and tearing the heel off a fresh loaf and pressing it into his hands without a word, still hot enough to hurt, and he would eat it standing up, and the smell of it, yeast and iron and woodsmoke.

He was seventeen now and had been working on this thing for two years. His prototype, he called it to himself. This project no one knew about. No one but Tuck.

Two years. Or, that was the official count. The truth was he had been working on something like the prototype for nine years, since he was eight years old, when he had taken apart the desk clock in the receiving room and put it back together upside down on purpose to see if his father would notice.

His father had not noticed for two months. When he had noticed, he had cuffed Burgundy on the side of the head and told him to leave the clock alone. Burgundy had taken it apart again that night and put it back the right way, and his father had never mentioned it again.

He thought, sometimes, about the miners. The people the planet ran on.

He did not know any of them. He could not have. He was the prince, and the prince did not go into the mines except to stand at the rim during inspections, in the embroidered coat his father made him wear, while the foremen read from their clipboards and the King nodded at whatever they said.

Once, only once, he had gone below the rim. He was twelve. The inspectors had opened a new corvite vein in the South 12 Shaft and his father wanted it shown to him, so they went down in the cage together, ringed by soldiers, half an hour of dropping through rock before the doors opened. South 12 was the deepest working on the planet. Tunnels forked off tunnels, decades of digging, and no one man alive knew the whole of it.

At the bottom the work lights ran along the walls in a long string, and the air was so thick with dust that the light looked like it was full of something solid. Lines of men were working the rock. Between the men, smaller and quicker, children, sorting and hauling the black crumbs that came off the wall. A smell came up from under everything, sulfur, strong enough to taste at the back of the throat. One of the attendants put a lozenge in Burgundy's hand, honey-colored, sweet, made to kill the smell, and when Burgundy looked at it too long the man tipped the tin and gave him a handful, and Burgundy put them in his coat pocket.

They walked the new vein while a foreman pointed and his father nodded. On the way back to the cage they passed a boy working a chisel into a seam at knee height. He was Burgundy's age, or near it. His hands were grey to the wrist, and the chisel had been ground down to half a chisel. Burgundy took one of the lozenges out of his pocket and put it in the boy's hand.

His father turned, slapped it out of the boy's hand, and crushed it under his heel. He told Burgundy he was not to do that. Then they walked on to the cage, and nothing else was ever said about it.

Five years now. The boy would be seventeen too, or near it. Burgundy did not know his name, or whether he was still on a chisel, or still in South 12 at all. He knew what the crews down there ate, because his tutor had told him once: greycaps, the pale fungus that grew along the walls of the deep mines, cut and dried and boiled into broth. On one visit to the rim a foreman had complained that the men spent their break hours crawling the side tunnels for greycaps instead of resting. That was the whole of what he knew about the boy's life. That, and the smell of the air he worked in.

The thing under the tarp traced back to him. A machine that could take the chisel out of his hands.

He had not told anyone this. He was aware that it was the sort of thing that would sound stupid if he said it out loud, I built it for a boy whose name I do not know, and he was aware that this was also possibly true.

And not only his. The prototype was one machine, but the notebooks under the bench held a hundred of them on paper: shaft plans, output tables, arithmetic he had run so many times he could recite it without looking, what the southern seams would give up if the cutting never had to stop and breathe. A third more ore inside a year, tooled properly, at scale. He had run the sum twice, then a third time, because he did not trust the first two.

His father ruled by a craft that had been handed down to him, the same shape passed along five times now, never explained, only inherited. Burgundy had a craft too, he had decided somewhere in the second year, and nobody had handed it to him. It could be measured. It could be proven on a page. When the crown came to him, and it would, the mines of Dross would not run on boys with chisels. That was not a wish. It was a number, and the number had his name on it.

He had not said any of that out loud either.

His father did not know about the basement.

Or, Burgundy hoped his father did not know about the basement. It was possible that he did.

His father was the King of Dross, a small planet that was mostly uninhabitable ice sheet, but that was packed with valuable ores.

"Our ores make travel between the stars possible" is something his father would say at the dinner table at least once a month. Whoever sat at the table heard it: Burgundy, a visiting dignitary, a governor, a minister. Burgundy didn't really understand what it meant. He never asked.

The tribute ships came every month and he had watched them for as long as he could stand at a window. Massive cargo vessels descending onto the planet to collect.

From that same window he could see the city. Kettletown. The planet's capital. It fell away from the castle walls in stacked tiers, houses built on the roofs of houses, iron sheeting gone orange with rust. In the warm months it looked like one great heap of salvage. In the cold months the snow came and settled over all of it and softened the rust to grey.

When he was very small he had asked his father what tribute was. What they loaded into the massive vessels. What were they taking away from our planet. Everything, his father had said. He didn't explain.

He had learned, eventually, the name of the man whose ships these were. He had never met the man. Nobody on Dross had ever met him. Nicholas was a name his father said the way other people talked about the season's rainfall, as a fact of the world that determined abundance or scarcity. It was all Nicholas.

The basement where he now worked had been a wine cellar once, before some great-great-uncle had decided wine was beneath royal dignity.

The bottles had been carried up and broken in the courtyard to make the point.

He had found the cellar when he was twelve and run the cables down himself, through a vent shaft.

Everything else in here he had scavenged: a motor left to rust in the servants' courtyard behind the kitchens, ball bearings from a broken serving cart, copper wire he stripped from a wall no one had lit in years.

And then he would work. Not for anyone. For himself.

He had told himself, sometimes, that he was preparing for something.

He had not been sure, until this year, what.

He ran the arm sequence from the console, the tarp still down. Fluid and smooth. It would be impressive tomorrow.

"Run it again," he said.

The panel brightened. A line of green resolved on the middle monitor, torque holding flat across the curve where last week it had collapsed. The machine gave the small tone it gave when a routine finished clean, one note, low.

"Good prototype," Burgundy said. He had never named it. Tuck's mom named everything: the boiler and the bread knife, her favorite cutting board, gave them all people-names, and Burgundy had decided years ago that naming objects was a way of lying to yourself. So he just said "prototype" to it.

"He's going to hate it," he said. And sighed a long breath.

The panel pulsed, slow, the way it pulsed when it was only waiting.

He had been careful in his rehearsals not to imagine his father laughing.

His mother had given him a glance, at the feast.

That was all. A glance. The birthday feast was the one dinner of the year held at the long tables in the great hall, and his seventeenth had filled them: governors down from the iron country, ministers and their wives in dresses that had crossed systems to reach Dross, the master of the eastern works refilling the King's cup before it was empty. Burgundy sat at his father's right hand and was looked at all evening by people who were actually watching his father.

Late in it, the finance minister stood with his cup and gave the toast everyone had come to hear. Three years to the Vicentennial, he said. Three years, and the prince to his first council in the morning, and the whole long work beginning. The hall drank to it. His father raised his cup at the toast and set it down and did not look at him.

And somewhere in the noise after, his mother looked at him across the table, briefly, and then looked away. An hour later, while a governor was telling the King a long story about grain, she did it again. He had spent his whole life noticing the things she did not say and trying to assemble them, and he had never quite managed it.

He was probably inventing it.

The code terminal blipped. The torque simulation had finished and produced an output graph he stared at for almost a minute before he understood what he was looking at. The graph was fine. The graph had been fine the last three times he had run it. He was running the simulation because he could not think of anything else to do, and he could not think of anything else to do because he was very tired.

He looked at his hand. Still shaking.

He looked at the timer in the corner of the monitor. The number had ticked over to thirty-two hours.

He should sleep. He knew he couldn't.

At the feast, a governor's wife had asked him, kindly, what came next for him, and then answered her own question before he could open his mouth. The training, of course. Then the rite, at twenty. Then the tour of the system. She had counted it off on her fingers. Everyone at that table could have. His whole life, said in one breath, by a woman whose name he never caught.

He was going to be king. That had never been the question. There was no other heir and no other outcome, and he did not lie awake wishing there were. What he lay awake on was the rest of it, the part that could be counted off on a stranger's fingers, a path worn pale before he was born.

The boiler coughed below.

Somewhere far above him, the palace clock struck three.

He laid his head down on his arms.

He told himself it was only for a moment. He told himself he just needed his eyes to stop hurting. He told himself a few more things, small reasonable things, and then, between one of those things and the next, he was no longer telling himself anything at all.

Chapter Two

"I Rule Men"

The Kings' Hall was the most used room in the castle. It was where all the decisions were made, mostly during morning council.

It was not beautiful. The King had spent his money elsewhere: a massive boulevard approaching the palace, rebuilt to be twenty lanes wide and flanked with carved stone giants of Burgundy's ancestors; or the stables, rebuilt twice, stocked with beasts from other systems. There was a private hunting ground, walled and kept and grain-fed the whole year round, so that the King could shoot one morning of it.

The hall did not get any attention. It was long, with an arched ceiling, stone ribs fanning out from the tops of the columns, and was mostly empty. The walls were the same grey stone as the floor.

Down the center of the long hall ran a single pale path, the stone worn a shade lighter by the feet of everyone who had ever walked from the huge entrance doors to the back side of the hall where the throne sat, atop a dais three steps up.

The hall did have one thing worth looking at. Behind the King's throne, a massive window ran nearly floor to ceiling. A thousand small panes of colored glass. And set among them, thin cut sheets of translucent stone, the rare kind pulled from Dross's deepest mines.

In daylight the stone panes would catch the sunlight and split it into gleaming threads of colored light in ways no glass ever could. And at night the stone glowed faint and pale like embers.

The window was the work of one of Burgundy's great-grandfathers, a king who had been obsessed with the stars. He had taken his cosmology from the Ghai, the ancient underground people who had lived here for a thousand years before Burgundy's clan conquered.

He built the window based on their reading of the sky: every pane placed so that the sun, moving through the seasons, would strike certain stones on certain days.

Each alignment meant something. The light on one stone marked the turning of the year; on another, a planting; on another, the harvest. What harvest? Burgundy always thought whenever his tutor would try to explain the cosmology of the big window to him pane by pane. He never asked out loud.

He had asked about Nicholas exactly once, when he was ten, in a geography lesson, in the flat bored voice he had already learned to use for the questions that mattered.

"Where does he keep his fleet," he said, not looking up from the star chart. "Nicholas. Between collections. Where do the ships go."

The tutor did not answer for a moment. When Burgundy looked up the man had gone still in a particular way, his stylus lifted off the slate and held there.

"We do not have that on the chart," the tutor said.

"But someone knows."

"Someone knows," the tutor agreed, and set the stylus down, and pulled out a map of the northern fields as though the northern fields were the most urgent thing in nine systems.

And there was no more talk of Nicholas.

On this particular morning, the first morning of his training, Burgundy had not planned to arrive late.

The council coat had come down from his rooms two nights before and hung since on the back of the basement door: heavy wool dyed the clan red, the clan's mark embroidered across the back and both shoulders in thread laid on so thick he could read the pattern with his thumb. He had planned to wake at five, wash, put it on properly, and walk the prototype up the basement stairs with an hour to spare, rehearsing what he would say when his father asked what the machine was. He had not planned to wake at seven, sleeve print on his face, the hours-counter showing thirty-six, his neck stiff in a way he could already tell would last all day.

He rushed to wash his face at the basement basin, and while he washed, the robot booted up across the room. In the thin morning light it looked smaller than it did at night. It stood on its one fat wheel, chest-high, the salvaged frame bolted and rewelded until the seams stood proud of the metal. Two arms, one for grabbing and one for drilling rock. The cutting bit at the drill arm's end was ground down to a dull working shine. Low on the front of the robot was a green-glass panel, where his code lived. This morning the panel pulsed slow and green, and Burgundy looked at the machine whole for the first time since he'd slept and thought, before he could stop himself, that it was either the best thing he had ever made or a toy, and that in a few hours his father would decide which.

The doors of the Kings' Hall had been shut to him at the council hour his whole life. He had walked past them a thousand times. This morning they stood open, because this morning, for the first time, he was expected through them. Nobody knew he was not coming alone.

He came up the last stair in the coat, still settling it across his shoulders, and walked in accompanied by a short robot on a single fat wheel that was proving to be quite loud against the stone. He had not noticed in the basement how loud it was. Down there the ventilation hum had absorbed the sound; up here, on the path of worn stone that ran from the doors to the throne, the wheel sounded like someone dragging a coin across a slate.

The boy and his robot walked toward the throne. His father sat at the far end, flanked by the long dark council tables filled with rows of advisors and stacked paper. The council was on a break. Advisors talked among themselves, their voices an echoing hum in the huge hall.

And behind them, the morning light was blaring through the great glass window, splashing colorful light across the floors and walls of the otherwise grey room.

As Burgundy got closer he caught a clear sight of his father on the throne talking to his security minister.

The King was a large man, broad through the chest, his brow set in a permanent seriousness, his hair going silver at the temples. Around his head he wore a plain band of gold, and set into it, small and unpolished, a strip of each of the eleven minerals the planet gave up.

When Burgundy had been very small he had thought his father was the largest thing in the world. He still thought so sometimes in dreams.

His mother stood beside the throne. Mira's hair was dust-grey, and she wore a gown the color of dried blood. She did not smile when she saw her son. Burgundy felt her watching him, and he searched her face for the look she had given him at the feast the night before. He could not find it. Her council face was on, and nothing in it moved.

As he arrived at the foot of the dais, the group started to go silent and look. The advisors stared, and Burgundy snuck a glance of their confused expressions; the one on the right had a smear of ink on his white collar he did not know was there. The one on the left looked at the prototype briefly and then away.

Burgundy's heart was ramping up.

"Burgundy," the King said, and the hall finished going quiet around the name. "Seventeen." He let the word stand a moment, like a number he wanted the room to look at.

Then his eye went past his son and landed on the machine, and stayed there.

"What is that."

Burgundy stepped forward and so did the robot. The wheel was loud again. Then it parked and lit up, the panel on its chest pulsed green, ready for the demonstration. It made a soft sound, almost like clearing its throat.

"Father," he said. "I want to show you something."

That was not the sentence he had rehearsed. The rehearsed one had numbers in it, and he reached for them now, because the numbers were the part of himself he trusted.

"It cuts rock. I ran it against a test face all year. One of these does the work of two men on a narrow seam, three on a bad one, and it doesn't tire, and it doesn't breathe the air down there. I built it from scrap. The parts cost less than a month of one miner's pay. Tooled properly, at scale, the southern shafts alone would give up a third more ore inside a year. I did the arithmetic twice."

The King looked at the machine longer than Burgundy had expected him to.

"Who taught you this," he said.

"Nobody. I taught myself."

"Nobody," the King repeated, and seemed to put the word away somewhere to be looked at later.

"How many men does one of them replace."

"Two." And, because it was true and he would not be caught rounding a number: "Three, on a narrow seam."

The King was quiet a moment. Then he turned, without any particular hurry, to the tables.

"Gentlemen." He said it easily, the way he said most things to that room. "My son tells me this will bring us a third more ore." He did not look back at Burgundy while he said it. "Perhaps he's right. We are already rich." He let that sit there, unraised, uncontested, and did not wait to see whether anyone smiled.

"It's not only the ore," Burgundy said, before he could stop himself. "It's what we could be, if the shafts ran different, if—"

"If." The King said it once, flatly, and the sentence had nowhere left to go.

He came down the three steps slowly, his coat brushing past the machine's shoulder the way a man brushes past a servant not worth stepping around, and stopped in front of his son, close now, and looked at him instead of it for the first time since he'd entered the hall.

"Ore was never the problem," he said, quieter now, this part not for the room. "There has always been more rock on Dross than men to cut it. Any clerk can count what comes out of the ground. This is where my father started me too." A pause, and Burgundy understood, too late, that it was not a pause for him to fill. "Do you know what it is I rule?"

He had heard the catechism before, somewhere, from someone, and the answer was in him, and it came out smaller than he wanted it to, the way a word had come out of him once at eight years old, holding a clock he had put back together upside down.

"Men."

"Men," the King said. "I rule men."

He let it stand a moment in the quiet hall.

And in the silence Burgundy became aware, all at once, of how much grease was on his hands. It was worked into the creases of his knuckles and under every nail, a night of it, two years of it. He curled his fingers into his palms and slid both fists into the pockets of the coat.

"A foreman walks a narrow seam three times a shift. Not to watch the rock come down. The rock comes down the same whether he's there or not. He walks it because three men working close, hands busy, eyes on the wall, that is when a man says a thing he would never say standing still. Who's short on his rent. Who's angry at his shift boss. Who's been asking questions he oughtn't." He looked past his son, at the machine, and then back. "You've built something that digs quietly and tells me nothing at all. I don't want quiet, Burgundy. I want to know."

"This planet has never been invaded," he said. "Not in five generations. Not because of the ore." He held his son's eyes, and there was nothing of anger in his face at all, only the flat certainty of a man repeating a thing he had said many times, each time as if for the first time to whoever was listening. "Because I know what is moving under it before it moves."

Nobody in the hall stirred.

Then he set his boot against the machine's wheel and pushed, not hard, the way a man tests a cart's brake with his foot, to see whether it holds. The prototype rocked back on its wheel, the green panel flickering once, and somewhere along the right side of the hall one of the advisors made a small, soft sound that might have been a cough and might not have been.

Burgundy heard it.

In the basement, in the long hours of the night, he had run every scenario he could imagine for how this conversation might go. He had imagined the yes. He had imagined the no. He had been careful, he remembered now being careful, not to imagine a laugh. He had not been able to bring himself to imagine a laugh.

But the sound had not come from his father.

It had come from an old man with ink on his collar, an old man whose name Burgundy was not sure he had ever known, an old man who had been standing in this room since before Burgundy was born.

"Take it away," the King said.

"Father—"

"I said take it away."

Then he turned from his son to the tables, and his voice opened out into the one he used for proclamations.

"Gentlemen. My son begins his training today."

He was looking at Burgundy while he said it, and he went on looking at him, and spoke to the room.

"For the next three years he will be schooled in the craft of ruling, as I was schooled, and my father, and his, in the tradition of the clan that has held this planet for five generations. When the training is done, on his twentieth birthday, at his Vicentennial, he will be crowned prince and heir in this hall, before all of you. And he will go out on the tour of the system, as every heir of this line has gone."

He stood over the machine without looking at it.

"The craft of ruling Dross is not a small craft. It asks for discipline. It asks for focus." And then, only then, back to his son, the proclamation dropping away and something flatter underneath it: "You will be here tomorrow morning. Without your toys. Ready to do what it takes to become the king of this place. We will call that the beginning."

Burgundy looked down at the prototype.

"Yes, sir," he said.

He did not look at Mira.

He did not look at the advisors.

He turned the prototype around, by hand, because he did not trust himself to give it a command in a voice that would not crack, and he pushed it back toward the doors. The wheel loud again as it moved across twenty colorful shades of sunlight. The hall behind him silent. Burgundy walked the length of the hall with his hands on the prototype's housing, and at the doors he did not stop, and he did not look back, and he pushed through into the corridor.

The doors closed behind him.

He stood for a moment with his hand on the prototype, in the long cold corridor where his footsteps would have echoed if he had taken any. He stood very still. His right hand was shaking again.

The prototype hummed beside him. Its green panel pulsed once, gently.

Chapter Three

Mira's Wing

Through the closed doors, the council resumed. His father's voice was a low rumble without shape. An advisor answered. Then his father again, longer, and something in it must have been a joke, because it got its soft, polite laughs.

He was still listening when the small side door in the corridor opened.

His mother slipped out.

She did not look at him. She closed the door behind her without a sound. She stood for a moment with her hand still on the handle, listening to the council, until the King said something else that was answered by the same polite noise, and then she turned to Burgundy, and she put one finger against her lips.

"Come," she whispered. It was less a word than a breath. "Bring it." Her eyes went to the robot.

Burgundy opened his mouth.

She shook her head, a single small motion, and turned, and began to walk down the corridor.

He put his hand on the prototype's housing. He pressed the small command that told it to follow. The prototype rolled after him.

They walked.

They took a corridor Burgundy knew, the long one that led past the council chamber toward the eastern courtyards. Then Mira turned right, into a smaller corridor that Burgundy had only walked twice in his life. And from here he began to lose his bearings.

She took him past a door he had believed was a broom closet. Behind the door was a narrow flight of stairs going up.

She started up them without hesitation.

Burgundy stopped at the foot of them. The prototype stopped behind him. Its wheel, a single fat wheel, salvaged from a serving cart, rated for stone floors and stone floors only, was going to have to climb stairs.

He had prepared for this, sort of.

Two months ago, in the basement, he had cut the wheel open. He had lined the inner rim with a rubber sleeve. He then wired a small air compressor into the chest cavity. He had coded a 'soft-mode' that would let the tire release air, enough for the wheel to deform around the edges of steps so it could climb them.

It had worked decently this morning as he took the robot up a few small sets of stairs from the basement to the council. But he had never taken it up a full spiral flight. He had not been sure he would ever need to.

He pressed the command.

The prototype's chest made a small hydraulic sound. The wheel visibly relaxed. It looked, for a moment, as though it had lost some air. Then Burgundy pushed it against the first step. The wheel deformed. It caught. He pushed. The prototype rocked forward, awkward like it was about to fall, but then it mounted the first step, and then the second, and then a rhythm settled into it that was almost a walk. It was climbing.

Mira was five steps up, watching.

"You really built this?" she asked quietly..

"Yes."

She nodded once and kept climbing.

Then, three steps further up, without turning:

"How long."

"Two years."

The stone at the top of the stairs was a different color, warmer, cream instead of grey, and the sconces along the walls were made of a metal he could not immediately identify, worked with a repeating pattern that looked, when he looked closely, like nothing he had seen anywhere else in the palace. The air smelled different too. Not the wet-stone smell of the palace's lower halls. It smelled like books.

Mira did not stop to let him look.

She took him through a small entry hall, and into a larger corridor with a high vaulted ceiling and windows on one side, all shuttered, all dusty. Their shoes made no sound; the floor here was carpeted, a long red runner going grey. Burgundy realized, walking down it, that the runner had hardly been walked on at all. It felt brand new.

"Mother, where —"

"Not yet."

They passed doors. Most were closed. One was open a crack, and through it Burgundy caught a glimpse of a sitting room, chairs still angled toward each other, but otherwise empty and gathering dust.

They turned a corner. They turned another.

Mira stopped at a set of double doors, pushed them open, and stepped through into a room that stopped him where he stood.

Burgundy had known there was a library in the palace. There were several. The one near the entrance, where his father sometimes met with foreign envoys, had a wall of leather-bound treaties and a globe that had not been updated in his lifetime. The one off the council chamber held the ledgers and the tax records and the tribute receipts. Neither of them was this.

This library was three stories tall and another two stories down, dug deep into the ground, all contained in this one massive room.

The room was cut up into a labyrinth of bookcases and crossings, stacks running into stacks, with narrow iron walkways bridging the gaps at each level so a person could walk from one wall of books to another without ever touching the floor. There were ladders on brass rails that traveled the length of each wall.

And doors. Dozens of them, small and arched, set into the shelving in no order Burgundy could find. One was half his height. Another only reachable from the iron walkway above.

A spiral staircase in the center connected the whole thing. Down at the basement level stood the tables. Long, dark, oiled, scattered with reading lamps and stacked books, and on one of them a small model of a star system Burgundy did not recognize. The table.

The first surveillance-free room on Dross. He didn't have the thought in words yet. He would, on the walk back.

"Skorn."

The hard-faced man did not nod. Names.

Names in a cramped, careful hand, column after column, page after page — names, and beside each name a shaft, and beside each shaft a date. He knew it.

He had seen it in a ledger, in the council archive, attached to a one-time expense in his grandfather's-era accounting that his father had once referenced approvingly: pacification and repair. The year before Burgundy was born. He had read the line eighteen months ago and filed it. "Halda."

The dry, upright woman looked at Burgundy the way a well looks at a bucket.

"I do supply," she said. "And I'll tell you what supply means, since you're learning tonight. "The people in the mines, prince, are the most watched people on this planet. Your machines can carry rock. Can they carry that?"

"Hesta."

It was Skorn. The first word he had spoken all night, and the room reorganized around it.

"Is this wise." He did not look away from Burgundy as he said it. "You're reading him our whole book. Names, methods, the stores, the leash — an hour ago the boy knew nothing that could hang anyone, and you're fixing that as fast as you can talk."

"I know what I'm doing, Skorn."

"He's the son."

"I know whose son he is." Hesta's voice stayed level. "And I know we're short on options, and I know one thing more, because I spent this week finding it out. I had his story walked back — every piece of it that touches ground we can see. The boy lies." Burgundy's stomach dropped, and she watched it drop. "He lies about small things. Where his hours go. Who packs his suppers. What he does with his nights. He has been lying to the whole palace for a year and a half, smoothly, and never once, in any of it that we could reach, has a lie of his cost anyone below him anything. That's a strange kind of liar, and it's the only kind I had any hope of sitting at this table." She turned back to Burgundy. "It isn't trust. Trust is years. It's a wager, and I'm the one placing it, and every soul here knows my stake is the same as theirs."

Skorn sat back. Not convinced — Burgundy could see it wasn't conviction, it was deferral, a man banking his objection where he could reach it fast — and Hesta went on as if the exchange had been an agenda item, which perhaps it had.

"Moss."

The slight man with the folded hands inclined his head a fraction of an inch.

"Moss served," Hesta said.

That was all. Two words, and the table went still in a way it had not gone still even for the tally-book — a stillness with weight in it, everyone briefly finding somewhere neutral to rest their eyes — and nobody asked a follow-up, and nobody offered one, and the two words did more work than Halda's whole ledger. Burgundy looked at Moss's hands, folded and motionless on the table. Precise hands. Quiet hands. He thought of the three thousand men in barracks by the shaft complexes, and the ledger term, and he did not ask either.

"And Wraven."

The girl had been on the table itself for some time — not at it, on it, cross-legged at the far end beyond the lantern, where she had drifted at some point no one had marked. She looked at Burgundy with the clear eye and the clouded one, and it was a frank pricing look, and it was Hesta's look, learned young.

"Tobin's niece," Hesta said. "His sister's girl. The sister's gone; Tobin raised her." Something crossed the big man's face and was banked, quick as Wraven crossing a lit space. "Two years she's run our signals, which makes her a veteran, and she'll tell you so. She goes where grown runners won't fit and thinks of things grown heads don't. She does it because she's a teenager and she can, and one day that math will stop working, and until it does she is the most surprising thing we have." Hesta paused. "You met, I believe."

"It doesn't get tired," Wraven said, to Burgundy, by way of confirmation. "Still true?"

"Still true," Burgundy said.

"Huh," said Wraven, and looked, briefly, satisfied — and fourteen — before the clear eye shuttered back to business.

Burgundy sat there with all of it in front of him — the book, the leash, the two words, the girl on the table — and asked the question that had been assembling in him for an hour, and heard, too late, how it sounded.

"Wait. So there's — a real movement?"

The table laughed.

All of them at once, even Halda, even, briefly, at one corner of his mouth, Skorn — and Tobin most of all, Tobin threw back his head and laughed from his boots, a huge open rolling sound, and it went UP, up past the lantern rings, up the columns and the climbing veins, up into the vaulted black where the five rivers of ore met, and the Hollow took the laugh and carried it around the dark overhead and gave it back multiplied, so that for a moment the dead kingdom itself seemed to be laughing at the prince of Dross — the first laugh, Burgundy somehow knew, that had gone up into that ceiling in four hundred years, and it was at his expense, and he found he did not mind, because a room that could laugh was a room that had decided, provisionally, not to hate him.

"Oh, boy," Tobin said, wiping his eye. "A real movement, he asks."

"There has always been a movement," Hesta said, and the laughter banked itself to embers. "That's the thing you were never taught, because teaching it is the one thing your father fears. What there is now is the latest movement. My uncle's was before it, and it ended in the square above South Six with the children made to watch. Skorn's grandfather's was before that — eleven days, and the book. Before that the Lantern March, in my mother's mother's time; before that the winter the gates were sealed; before that, before that." She recited it without drama, a crew chief reading a shift roster of the dead. "Every generation rises. Every rising is crushed. Half this table is the children and grandchildren of the crushed — that's not misfortune, that's selection; the crown breeds its own opposition by hanging our families in front of us. And each time it's crushed the same way. Not by the army. The army is the last step, the cheap step. It's crushed by the watching — the stores, the ledgers, the informers, the raids that come at night with no charge and no record, the people who go into the dark and don't come out and are never spoken of by anyone who wants to keep their own. Zero tolerance, perfectly enforced, forever. Your father doesn't run a mine that treats people badly, boy. He runs a system whose product is people who can't lift their heads — and the ore pays for it."

Burgundy sat still.

He was hearing double, and the doubling was making him faintly sick. Every phrase she said had a twin he already knew. The raids that come at night — the interior minister's networks addressed. The people who go into the dark — six men taken, the taking kept quiet. The army, the last step — pacification and repair. Perfectly enforced — quiet plus festival; a man with something to look forward to does not burn down what he has; time it right and you are never more than a few weeks from safety. He had learned the whole machine already, at the left hand of the machinist, nodding, filing, telling himself it was reconnaissance. He had had every fact. He had had all of it except the direction he was hearing it from, and the direction, it turned out, was everything — and sitting still under it got harder with each sentence, and he made himself sit still, because listening was the only honest thing he had brought to the table.

When she finished, nobody spoke for a while. The lanterns fluttered in some slow deep current of air that had been circling the Hollow since before the palace had foundations.

"So," Hesta said. "Now you know who the people in the mines are. Make your offer."

He made it smaller than the speech he'd prepared, and better.

"The machines can carry your quota," he said. "Every shift. You saw it — two hours, and that was three of them showing off on a rich face. Give them a working face and your crew's lungs never touch rock dust again. Your stipends stay whole because the weight is always in. The small ones stay out of the pinch seams." He looked down the table, at each of them, and ended at Hesta. "I can't fix what you've just taught me. I'm not going to pretend tonight that I know how. But I can take the mine off your backs while you — while you do whatever it is you do. That much I can build. That much is already built."

It landed. He could see it land — Tobin nodding slowly, Halda doing supply arithmetic behind her dry eyes, Maren watching the lanterns, Wraven watching him. It landed on every face but one.

"Easing the burden," Skorn said, "is what the festivals are for."

The sentence went into Burgundy like something thin and cold, precisely because it was not an insult. It was a classification. Skorn had listened to the offer and filed it, accurately, on the shelf where the regime kept its own instruments — the drink, the drums, the three suspended days, all the machinery of making the unbearable bearable so that it would go on being borne. You are offering to make the mine sufferable, the sentence said. The mine being sufferable is how the mine survives. And Burgundy had no answer, because four days ago he would not even have understood the charge, and tonight he understood it perfectly and could not yet see to the bottom of it — and Skorn watched him not-answer, and nodded once, minutely, as if the not-answering were the first honest data the boy had produced all night.

"We'll take it," Hesta said.

Skorn's head turned.

"We'll take it as what it is," she said, to Skorn as much as to Burgundy, "which is a first installment. He doesn't know what it's a first installment on. That's all right. He'll learn or he won't." She turned back to Burgundy, and it was all logistics now, brisk as a shift assignment: "Not the main face — the machines go to the old Deep West workings, off level nine. Inspectors haven't walked it in six years; there's a reason, the corvite's thin out there, but thin will still make quota if your machines are what tonight says they are. And they pack their harvest like men did it — pull them back, whatever it takes. Rough the loads. Mix the grading. Nothing honest ever looked as clean as those carts did tonight; another train like that one and the assay office starts asking who found a new seam, and then men with instruments come down my shaft." She paused, and something moved behind the flat eyes that was not quite visible from the outside. "And the crew works no rock. Quota lands, lungs stay clean — and the shift has ten hours a night it has never had in any generation of any family at this table. We'll see what the crew does with that."

She said the last sentence to the room, and the room heard it, and nobody at the table looked at anybody else, which was its own kind of look.

Maren rose, and began taking down the lanterns.

The party broke up the way it had assembled — into the dark, by ways he was not shown. Maren gathered her coil of lanterns, lit the last one for the road as she had lit the first for the room, and led the four away up a tunnel Burgundy could not afterward have located. Hesta gave him the route back as far as the junction and the time of contact for delivering the machines — through Tobin, everything through Tobin now — and then she was gone too, and Tobin gripped his hand once, and said, "You did all right, for a prince," and was gone after her.

Burgundy was ten steps into the dark of his own road home when Wraven appeared at his elbow.

She did it without sound — one moment tunnel, next moment girl — and she walked beside him for a few paces, hands in her pockets, matching his stride in the loose loping way of someone who had grown too fast for herself, and he waited, because he had learned tonight that this table spoke when it chose to.

"They did the arguing about whether you're a spy," she said. "That's done, so I won't. I just have the one thing."

"All right."

She stopped, so he stopped. In the lantern light the milk-glass eye caught and held like a small moon, and the clear one fixed on him, and when she spoke her voice was not the table's voice, not doctrine, not history — it was just young, and level, and completely without protection.

"For you this is a thing with your father," she said. "Whatever happens, at the end of it you're still a prince. They don't hang princes." She let that sit exactly one beat, Hesta-trained. "It's not like that for anyone else you met tonight. It's Tobin. It's everyone I know. Every single one, and their kids, and their mums." A small shrug, terribly light. "So. Just — don't screw us over. That's all. That's the whole thing."

And she was gone — up some crack in the dark that a grown runner would not have fit — and Burgundy stood alone in the dead kingdom with his lantern and the weight of the most unanswerable brief he had ever been handed, delivered in under a minute by a fourteen-year-old, and then he walked home, and the walk home was where it happened.

---

It assembled the way his breakthroughs assembled. He knew the feeling intimately — the sensation of scattered readings suddenly agreeing to be one curve — and he had learned long ago not to interrupt it, so he walked, lantern swinging its small room of light along the old stone, and let it come.

He had spent his whole life believing the problem was inefficiency.

That was the given he had never once examined. The mines were brutal because the tools were bad; the tools were bad because his father was incurious; therefore: build better tools. Every night in the basement, every disc, every prototype — all of it rested on that footing. Bad conditions were the malfunction. He was the repair.

But the system was not malfunctioning.

He turned the evidence over the way he turned a log stream, and it all pointed one way. The stores that made sure everyone owed. The listening walls built where the queues stood. The festivals timed like pressure valves — cheaper than the army, and it works better. The army itself, budgeted beside the tribute as the same expense. The stipends shaved to the exact edge of endurance and never past it. None of that was neglect. Neglect is noisy, random, wasteful — and this had no noise in it anywhere. It was engineered. It was maintained. It was, he made himself say in the privacy of his own skull, well built — four generations of careful iteration, every component load-tested, his father's craft laid over his grandfather's — a machine whose product was not ore.

The ore was the pretext. The product was people who could not lift their heads.

And then the last piece came, the way the last piece always came — sideways, quietly, already inevitable — and it stopped him where he stood.

He had been about to make the machine more efficient.

That was what his offer was. That was what it was, beneath the clean lungs and the whole stipends: quota met at lower cost, output steady, the southern shafts running better than ever — every improvement he fed into the mine flowed up the same pipe and came out as the regime's strength. A richer mine bought more informers, funded the barracks, paid the festival allotments, fattened the tribute that kept Nicholas's ships overhead. Skorn had seen it in four seconds. Easing the burden is what the festivals are for. He, Burgundy, builder, was standing in the supply chain of the thing he hated, offering to optimize it — and the help these people needed, the only help that was not ultimately a festival, was not a better mine.

It was no regime.

He stood absolutely still in the tunnel, in the small room of lantern light, between — he was aware of the geography with a terrible new clarity — his mother's white room somewhere above him at the end of this road, and his father's palace above that, the whole weight of it resting on the dead kingdom he stood in, and he let himself look directly at the thought for one full second:

Real help means the end of it. And I am its heir.

He did not finish the thought. Not all the way — not to the place the thought was plainly going, the place with a shape and a date and a word he was not ready to hold even alone in the dark a kilometer under everyone he knew. He was an engineer; he knew what it meant when a curve left; he knew you did not have to see a line's end to know its direction.

He picked the lantern up — he had not noticed setting it down — and walked the rest of the way home, and told no one anything, and lay awake a long time, not with excitement now.

With arithmetic.

— RETAINED FROM THE OLD PLAN, all live, adapted to the showing: Mira's anger as a broker's damage assessment ("You did not come here to ask. You came here to collect."); the counter-math walk, margin arithmetic out loud, arriving despite herself at interest; the pivot: "My family has waited four generations for Dross to have something worth more than its ore. It has one now. It has a machine that makes machines."

— THE PIPELINE ARCHITECTURE, negotiated line by line (keep concrete and limited): OUT: surplus corvite only, pressed bundles, capped at one part in forty of official tonnage ("the assay noise floor"; the cap is Mira's, non-negotiable: greed is how smuggling dies). IN: hardware distributed through existing cargo runs, never a dedicated shipment, never a pattern. THE CHAIN, every link a person, every person a family: her weigh-clerk at the cargo field (hers, twenty years); a logistics checker in the mine transport pool to be recruited (Toby's cousin; knits to Halda's webs); the night crew at the shaft end. She makes him recite the chain back and name what happens to each link if it breaks. COMMS: nothing written that isn't burned; the breakfast-book method retired (too regular, too long-running); new dead-drop rotation.

— THE STUDY: she takes him deeper, into the correspondence room he has never seen (even in her domain there are layers): the equipment, the coded ledgers, two decades of traffic. Shows him ONE ledger page, the family's Dross accounts, as reciprocal exposure: "Now we have each other's necks. That is the only contract that holds." (Echo of the Vein arrangement, same joint at every scale; visible to the reader, invisible to Burgundy.)

— CLOSING: weeks compressed; first surplus out under the tribute's nose; first real hardware order back in; Mira watching him inventory it, a private held breath: she has just bet the family's oldest channel on her son. OPEN CALL: the optional two-paragraph Mira-interiority coda (first departure from strict Burgundy-close; decide deliberately).⟧

Chapter 25

The Rhythm

— PURPOSE: the year montage, ~10-12 months. Sibling of the Double Life montage, inverted emotionally: community in secret, ascending flow. It should feel GOOD while the narration plants costs and counts.

— ALL PREVIOUS RHYTHM CONTENT RETAINED: the template night told once in full (shift bell; Drekkos against the vein; quota landed by hour two; the shift TURNS: thirty miners become builders, Ghai galleries strung with work-lights, Burgundy teaching soldering to hands that held picks thirty years; miners' hands perfect for fine assembly; Toby running the build-floor; Hesta running quality; Wraven cloned onto the pin-tag system, machines trailing him like hounds).

— THE TEACHING DOCTRINE: chalk diagrams become stenciled plates; build-teams score their own output; nobody says out loud that he has turned the crew into a feedback loop; the narration says it once, dryly.

— THE PIPELINE PULSE: bundles out with ore trains, crates in under manifest, the weigh-clerk's chalk mark, the cousin's checkboard. ONE NEAR-MISS mid-chapter: an unscheduled assay audit; the crew feeds the auditors a hard-luck vein; the machines deliberately under-mine nine days to reset the curve; Skorn's idea; everyone notices it is the machines' first taught LIE.

— THE COUNT, the chapter's clock and menace, italicized drumbeat: eleven machines / twenty-six / forty and the first gallery outgrown / seventy / one hundred and four, and the old kingdom's lamps were lit again.

— THE UNLOCK, mid-montage set-piece slowed to full scene: machines that build machines. Burgundy watches a miner's assembly motion, re-tasks two Drekkos with manipulator arms; the loop does the rest; the first machine-built machine off a Ghai-stone bench with tolerances no human hand can hit; the build rate breaks its human ceiling. Mark it with the same quiet terror as the mining curve leaving. HESTA present; her one unsentimental line lands the scene: "It doesn't need us." His non-answer echoes his non-answer to Skorn.

— DIVERSIFICATION, fast: haulers, lifters, a shoring unit better than the Ghai's, the small mapping crawler (siege map seed). The under-country alive: lit galleries, machine sounds in tunnels dead four hundred years, meals at Ghai tables.

— ADJUSTMENTS TO FIT THE NEW SPINE: the line scales FROM the ch10 little factory (continuity of place and crew); Hollow meetings and suppers continue as background pulse, one or two feast-beats, cooler and more workmanlike (the group has become an operation; narration notices the temperature without mourning yet); the six in working roles: Toby/floor, Halda/supply-and-people, Moss/tooling (army knowledge sheathed), Wraven/machines-and-signals, Skorn/security and hard questions, Hesta/everything.

— DAY-LIFE COUNTER-PULSE: council, festivals, the King's increasing warmth, consulting his son. The irony ceiling: Burgundy at council auditing the SOUTHERN SHAFT PRODUCTION numbers his own conspiracy fakes; his father, pleased: best-run shaft on the planet, whatever they are doing down there the others should learn it.

— CLOSING: the night the count passes one hundred; no ceremony; a lit gallery, two kinds of labor indistinguishable in sound, tunnels carrying it; he stops at the newest machine-built Drekko, looks at the manipulator arms, and has the thought he has refused all year: an arm that can hold a welding head can hold other things. One line. Cut.⟧

Chapter 26

Other Things

— PURPOSE: the moral hinge. Burgundy decides to weaponize; the conspiracy fractures along old lines (Skorn now FOR, the inversion pays off); Mira asked for the one thing she has never moved: weapons. Three confrontations in rising order of cost: Hesta, the crew, Mira.

— PRECIPITATING EVENT (open call, proposals stand): the festival cycle brings another crackdown, a fourth network broken, the taken now one degree from the crew (the hesitant oath-taker's brother from the north shafts, if the oath scene is resurrected, else a named build-captain's kin); council's own numbers show the interior ministry requesting army growth; the regime arming for the thing it feels coming. Alternates: a foreman too near the lagging wall, bought or scared; an army sweep through the workers' quarter mid-shift.

— HESTA FIRST (deliberate order: not Toby who'd say yes from loyalty, not Skorn who'd say yes from anger; the one who will interrogate it). Her position: a machine that fights makes every miner a combatant the day it's discovered; the regime treats organizing as war already; this hands them the proof. His position: the count's half-life; discovery is a WHEN; on that day the crew is defended or dead. Neither wins. Her closer: "Build it. Build one. And understand that the day you build the second one, you've decided something you're pretending you haven't."

— THE CREW VOTE, now IN THE HOLLOW (the feast room hosting the war vote; let the setting do that arithmetic silently). Skorn argues FOR with the specificity of a man who has buried friends, standing on the tally-book he laid on the table in ch9; his speech disturbs Burgundy because it mirrors his own private reasoning with the humanity left in. MOSS UNSHEATHES: his one-sentence thaw pays off; he specs what the army actually carries. The vote carries; give the dissenters names and let them STAY (movements are made of people who lost the vote and stayed).

— THE ASK, MIRA (the summit): numbers first, like every presentation he has made her. Her refusal immediate and NOT moral: professional, which is worse: electronics deniable, ore deniable, weapons a different crime in every code the cartel operates under, a different price when caught, and, arrived at last, the real reason: weapons on Dross means her son is planning to use them on her husband. She says what no one has said in twelve chapters: "You are building an army to kill your father." His answer, written with total control, neither denial nor confession: "I'm building an army so that when the day comes, it's a choice and not a massacre." She recognizes her own language in her son's mouth; recognition, not persuasion, is why she yields.

— HER CONDITION (bridge to ch15): if weapons move through family channels, THE FAMILY MUST KNOW what it is moving toward; she will not smuggle her relatives blind into a coup. He resists (the circle widens again; the Broker argument replayed with roles reversed, make the reversal explicit). Mira: "You taught me this rule. Necks, both ways."

— THE THREE WEEKS: compressed build, tight and cold where the Rhythm was warm: the first weapons crate (small arms, armor plate, two things Burgundy doesn't recognize and Mira won't gloss); a gallery the crew starts calling, without deciding to, THE ARMORY; the build itself: machines building their successor, Burgundy specifying instead of soldering; he barely touches it; that detail should ache.

— CLOSING IMAGE: Battle Drekko V1, contra-genre: no gleam, no awe. Armored, mounted, patient, green panel pulsing the same rhythm as every mining unit; the horror is that it is NOT different: same loop, same patience, same politeness, new task. Wraven looks at it longest. Final line candidate: the machines had learned everything else in a week; no one said out loud how long this one would need.⟧

Chapter 27

Proof

— SETTING: a deep Ghai gallery converted to a proving range, far enough down that four hundred meters of dead kingdom swallow the sound.

— THE TEST: V1 against targets the crew builds: timber barricades, plate mock-ups. MOSS AND SKORN spec the mock-ups (Moss's inside knowledge of army equipment now explicit; Skorn's expertise is scars). The machine gets the task and no method; the loop finds methods. The improvement WITHIN the single session: first engagement clumsy, tenth clean, twentieth showing a solution nobody taught and nobody foresaw. The room's silence changes quality: the third time the curve leaves.

— THE REACTIONS, walked in turn: Skorn vindicated and, surprise, quieter than anyone (the thing he wanted for a decade exists and is not warm to stand next to). Toby re-costing every friend in the army's path. Hesta watching BURGUNDY, not the machine (re-pricing arc, penultimate mark). Wraven asks the avoided operational question: "How does it know who?"

— THE FRIEND/FOE SCENE (thematic keystone, spend care): the answer is the pin-tag network scaled: crew tagged, machines reading tags. Technically clean. Then one character (Hesta or Wraven) states the shadow: everything not tagged is, to the machine, a target category. The pin, small and warm, given like a gift all act, revealed as the whole moral architecture in miniature: inside the loop or outside it. This is the act's quiet answer to the what-happens-when-he-doesn't-need-us question, and it is not comforting. (Flag: this system is BUILT to break: an untagged ally, a tagged traitor, a lost pin; where it breaks is an Act II chapter waiting.)

— CLOSING, minimal: the range clears; Burgundy alone gives the workshop queue its next order. The narration does not editorialize. Hesta's "the day you build the second one" detonates in the reader's memory unassisted. Cut.⟧

Chapter 28

Family Business

— THE LETTER THAT CANNOT BE A LETTER: open on craft: how to signal "possible regime change, prepare accordingly, business continues or improves" across months of disc-lag, in a code surviving interception by Nicholas's people AND the family's rivals. Mira composing: drafts burned, the family cipher, the house's old trade idiom: management change at the mine; new foreman, same ore; recommend the family increase its position. Burgundy watching her work and realizing, one line, that he is watching where he comes from.

— THE SECOND LAYER: she is not informing, she is POSITIONING: securing the family's backing for her son's regime before it exists, which is also offering the family a stake in it. Burgundy catches it and challenges: is he mounting a revolution or being acquired? Her answer honest and insufficient: "You needed weapons. Weapons come with owners." DO NOT RESOLVE. (Mirror now complete: what happens when the prince doesn't need the miners / what happens when the cartel doesn't need the prince.)

— RESSA IN PERSON (recommended; first cartel face on the page): down with a cargo run. Written against Mira: same patience, none of the concealment; a woman who has never had to pretend to be less than she is. Inspects the operation (tunnels, the count, the V1s, now plural) with a professional's eye; delivers the family's terms with a smile that has numbers in it: the family moves the weapons, absorbs the surplus, recognizes the new regime, ON CONDITION of first-position trade rights in the post-Vorran order, and one more condition, delivered lightly, landing like a stone: the family will want to understand the machines. Eventually. At a time of its choosing. Burgundy hears "a machine that makes machines" come back with interest attached. Mira does not push back. (Seed of seeds: everyone will want the loop. Nicholas, when he finally turns his head this way, will want the loop. Act II's sky is in this clause.)

— THE EVE INVENTORY, quiet and wide, the act taking stock the way its montages taught the reader to read: a hundred-plus machines and the armory growing; a crew that is a trained cadre; a dead kingdom mapped palace-to-shafts; a pipeline breathing weapons; a queen with her family committed; a king who believes his son has never been more his; a festival coming; six men still in the dark; pressure the festivals no longer bleed. COUNT THE NEW ASSETS TOO: the Hollow, the six, the food-chain, Pell, the veyl economy: the human machine assembled beside the mechanical one.

— FINAL SCENE (open call, proposal): Burgundy alone in the lowest gallery at the crawler's finished map: the whole under-country inked on one sheet, palace foundations at one edge, South Twelfth at the other, the thin marked route between them. He stands where the Ghai stood. Final lines rhyme with the book's opening: a boy who could not sleep, awake in the dark, with a plan where the timer used to be. Cut to black. End of Act I.⟧
