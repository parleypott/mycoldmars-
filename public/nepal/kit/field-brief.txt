## A. THE THEME

### The sentence

**"The road brings everything in and carries everyone away."**

Say it every morning. Every scene proves it, complicates it, or gets cut.

The dinner version, the one a viewer retells to a friend: *"A new road finally reached Nepal's last roadless district, and it stocked a woman's shelves with everything except her family."* Shorter: **full shelves, empty house.** If the cut can't be retold in that sentence, the cut has drifted. Docs live on retelling.

You don't have to find this thesis in-country. Jaba states it unprompted in the scout clip, two consecutive breaths: "it's much easier now with roads" and "our kids are not here, our family is separated." Your audience thread said the same thing from the Alps, quoting Cognetti: "the roads that represented the promise of a better life they actually emptied the mountains." Your job is to keep the thesis load-bearing for 40 minutes. Nile stated its thesis at [01:38] and went dark for eight minutes. Stating it once is not threading it.

**The complication you must also shoot:** nobody here mourns the trail. People carried crushing loads for days, Jaba's own words, "ferrying the loads up and down mountains." Rice fell from airplanes for decades because nothing could climb the hills. The road was wanted, and the road takes something. Both are true at once.

### The universal noun

**SEPARATION.** Not roads, not development, not geopolitics. The road is the instrument; separation is the subject, and every viewer has someone far away because of an economic road of their own. Jiro's sushi carried mastery. Jaba's store carries separation. The women who stay, Jaba, the woman at 1:03, the nuns, are the camera's answer to it.

VO register, when you need it: the road converts distance from an experience into a delay, and the conversion is never total, because the mountain keeps the older clock and lends the road out season by season. That language beats any infrastructure statistic.

### Theme architecture (the Bourdain thread)

State it in the cold open, complicate it for forty minutes, return to it transformed. Between the bookends, restate it in the field, on camera, to a person, once per act (M9). This is the fix for the thesis-goes-dormant fault in all three prior cuts.

**Cold open, primary.** The scout two-shot of Jaba and her husband behind the counter, then the same frame today with her alone, then her line: "much easier now with roads... our family is separated." Thesis stated by the subject, in her voice, inside 90 seconds. You can't shoot the reshoot until D4, so protect the composition (see B). Backup: the blacktop edge at Pilchaur, the exact meter where pavement stops. Third, if the road performs: whatever blocks you, phone footage admissible.

**Field restatements, scheduled not hoped for:**
- Act 1 (the drive up, D2), to a driver or road worker: "Everyone tells me this road is the best thing that ever happened here. So why does every village I pass have no young men in it?"
- Act 2 (Simikot, D4), to Jaba, late in the interview: "You told me the road made things easier. You also told me it took your family. Which is bigger?"
- Act 3 (Hilsa, D6), on the bridge: "I came here asking who this road is for. Standing here, I'm not sure the answer is anyone on this side of the river."

**Close the loop, D7, the Kathmandu apple market.** The ending logic: an apple's price is not in the apple, it is in the road. The road will have worked on the day something climbs down it for money. The bitter rhyme underneath, said only if the footage earns it: the first thing that climbed down for money was the men. Do not end on the vendor. End on a person's face, Jaba's if you have the shot, and try cutting to black off the person before writing any conclusion VO (M8). Myanmar's ending was complete before the VO card.

### The clocks (all live, pick visibly)

1. **Apple harvest.** Happening now. The crates are a countdown.
2. **The road itself.** Chharebhir slid Sep 20, departure day. The road opening and closing IS the drama. When it closes, everyone becomes a walker again within the hour.
3. **Your own 8-day passage.** Night driving is banned (confirm with drivers D1), so darkness ends your traveling day exactly as it ended the caravans'. Use that on camera.
4. **Dashain.** Weeks away. The men come home once a year with cash and new clothes, then the buses run south again. Every woman you interview is inside this countdown. Ask about it.
5. **Winter.** Taklakot workers return by mid-December. The passes and the border close. The region is racing the cold.

## B. SITTING WITH JABA

This is the episode. Everything else is corridor. Read this section twice before D3.

### What you already have

1. **She states the thesis unprompted.** "We had to carry the goods for others and ourselves... it's much easier now with roads... our kids are not here, our family is separated." The scout clip is her rehearsed answer, comfortable, told before. Don't get the summary again. Get UNDER it.
2. **She smiles over the wound.** On "our family is separated" she is smiling, sometimes laughing. Same move as the Palau homeowner's smiling "there's no choice," your recurring missed door. This time the door is marked. The smile IS the interview. The second question behind the smile is the episode.
3. **The husband is gone now.** He stands beside her in the scout footage; he has since left. The archive holds a two-shot that reality emptied. That composition is waiting for its rhyme.
4. **Her shelves are the theme in object form.** Coca-Cola, noodles, Nescafe, biscuits, sacks of staples. Every visible object arrived on the road. She sits framed by the road's blessings while naming its cost. She used to carry this cargo on her back. Her store is her biography.
5. **Her youngest son works in Surkhet.** Where your shoot begins. The road physically connects your Day 1 to her front door.

### The Surkhet move (decide before Day 1)

Your only Surkhet window with slack is D1 evening. You haven't met Jaba, so contact goes through the fixer: fixer calls Jaba before you leave Kathmandu, asks permission, gets the son's number, asks if he's willing. If yes, one hour on D1: film him at his work (hands, tools, the job, shot like Planet Earth), a short question set, and a recorded video message to his mother on your phone. You carry it up the road for three days and play it for her on D4, phone to phone, in her store. You and the road did the same job that week.

This is the engineered retellable setpiece, the Merry Clayton moment: a mother hearing her son's voice carried up the road that took him. Protect it, over-resource it, hold the frame on her face while she watches. Whatever she does, smile or tears: count ten seconds, then the second question (M3). Ask her permission on camera before playing anything. If she says no, the refusal is also a scene. If either party hesitates at any point, drop it without ceremony; a declined setpiece costs nothing, a pushed one costs the film. Fallback runs the other way: record HER message on D4. But the book only fixes a D7 morning flight and the 2pm KTM market and does not confirm Surkhet ground time, so check routing with the fixer; if there is no window, send her message phone-to-phone from Simikot and film her sending it. The sending is the scene either way.

Son questions, D1: What do you do here? When did you leave Simikot? How did you travel then, and how long did it take? How long now? When did you last see your mother? Will you go home for Dashain? What would you tell her right now? *(That last one is the message.)*

### First contact, D3, 7pm

Protected first contact, the Bourdain rule. You have never met her; keep it that way until the camera rolls. No warm-up chat that flattens the real meeting. Arrive as a customer. Buy something, pay for it, drink it there. Let her run her counter, her stage and her confidence. She invites you to something (the book says she will). Say yes to whatever it is. **No interview on night one.** Eat, drink the tea, let the crew be boring. The Chef's Table law: the real confession comes around day eight of ten. You have three days; compress the trust curve by removing pressure, not adding it.

Trust moves that cost nothing:
- Learn her name and her sons' names from the fixer before you arrive.
- Let her show you the store before any camera does. Ask what she's proud of.
- Tell her the truth about the film early, one sentence, through the translator: "I'm trying to understand what the road gives and what it takes, and everyone says you're the person to ask." Locals in your thread wrote long, proud takes; treat her as a co-author and she will give you the film.

### The translated-interview discipline

One clause per question, no compounds, no idioms, no stacked hypotheticals. Translator renders answers verbatim, repetitions and hesitations included, not summaries. Camera on HER face during the Nepali answer, not the translation; the emotion happens the first time. Brief the translator on the second-question rule beforehand: after a heavy answer you will wait, and the translator must not fill the silence either. Ten seconds of quiet in a translated interview feels like a minute. Hold it anyway.

### The questions, in escalating order

**Warm, concrete, in the store, standing, handheld (D4 morning):**
1. What sold the most in the store this week?
2. (Holding one item) Where does this come from? How does it get here? What did it cost before the road?
3. Before the road, how did these goods get here? What was the heaviest load you ever carried? How many days of walking? *(She measures the old world in loads and nights. Let her. Then: will you show me how you carried a load?)*
4. Tell me about the day the first truck reached Simikot. Where were you standing?
5. The road closed this week at Chharebhir. When that happens, what happens to your prices? To this town?
6. Who in Simikot has the road helped most? Who has it helped least?

**The family, entered through facts (seated, quieter, when the room has earned it):**
7. Tell me about your sons. What are their names, what are they like?
8. What does your youngest do in Surkhet? When did he leave? Do you remember the morning he left? What did you cook?
9. Where is your husband right now, today? When did he leave? What was the last thing you two did together?
10. When they call you, what do you talk about? What do you not talk about?
11. What do you cook now, when it is only you? What is the house like at night?

**The thesis, turned (only after a raw line has landed and been held; likely late D4 or D6 evening):**
12. You told the last crew the road made life much easier. You also said your family is separated. Which one do you think about more?
13. You're smiling while you say that. Why? *(THE question of the shoot. You always skip it. Do not skip it. Do not ask it early.)*
14. If the road had never come, where would your sons be right now?
15. If giving back the road would bring your sons home, would you give it back? *(Sit in whatever answer comes. Do not rescue her, or yourself, with the next question.)*
16. Is there anyone in Simikot who thinks the road was a mistake? *(Your route to the one who gave up, M5. Follow the name she gives you.)*

**The future, the clock:**
17. Will they come home for Dashain? What happens in this house when they do? *("They are coming" is hope in the film. "Not this year" is the wound. Either answer is the episode.)*
18. If your son said tomorrow, "I'm coming home for good," what would you tell him? *(Her honest answer may be "don't." Sit in that.)*
19. Who gets this store one day?

Two spares for the end, only if earned: *Would you leave, if you could?* and *What should I tell people about this place?*

**Exit questions, last thing every visit:** "What did I not ask that I should have?" and "Is there anything you don't want in the film?" The Fallon test governs the edit: will she think you understood her?

**The second-question discipline, restated because you will forget in the moment.** Raw line: hold the face, count ten seconds, then ask the smaller question INTO the wound, never away from it. Not "how does that make you feel." Ask "what was that day like," "what did you cook that night," "where were you standing." Meadu's "It's over. It's the dam." got cooking pots within two seconds. Never again. Flag those takes in your notes as NO-VO.

### Scenes to shoot WITH her, not at her

1. **The store inventory walk.** Shelf by shelf, the story of five objects: what it is, what it costs, how it got here, what it cost before the road, what she had to do to get it then. Shoot it twice: once as conversation, once silent and tight, hands, labels, the ledger, the scale, dust. Planet Earth on a shop shelf. This carries the entire economics of the episode without a graphic.
2. **The scout-frame reshoot.** Pull up the scout clip on your phone. Set the exact two-shot composition, her behind the counter, the space beside her empty. Shoot it clean, hold it long, explain nothing. Then, separately: show her the old clip on camera and film her watching it. Ask nothing for a beat. Then: "What do you see?"
3. **She teaches you something.** The load-carry with the forehead strap, or how she cooks what she actually eats (lakkad, the buckwheat pancake, is the true staple, and the airlift rice made people ashamed of it; ask her about that), or the traditional pipe from the book. You be bad at it. Her laughing at you is bonded footage that belongs to her, not a giggle exit imposed on her, and it earns the hard questions. M6 applies: what is it, how is it made, what did her mother make, what do her sons miss most. Food questions are family questions wearing an apron.
4. **The empty rooms.** Ask her to show you the house. Whose bed was whose. What the husband left behind, what the sons left behind. Do not hunt for objects, do not ask her to perform grief. Shoot what she shows you tight, in daylight (lens rule); a jacket on a hook can carry more than any interview answer. If she goes quiet, the quiet is the scene. Phone rolls if the main camera feels heavy in there.
5. **The phone.** How does she talk to them? Show me. If she calls the son while you are there, that call outranks everything else on the schedule.
6. **The sit-down telling (D6 evening, after Hilsa).** Octopus Teacher was dead until Foster told the whole story fresh to a first-time listener. One evening, have her tell her whole story, marriage, loads, road, departures, start to finish, to YOU, the first-time listener you genuinely are. By D6 you have driven the entire road she depends on; tell her what you saw and let her correct you. That recording is your spine insurance.
7. **Ending-shaped moments (M8), shot on purpose:** her watching a loaded truck pull out of Simikot; closing the shutters at dusk, the lamp going on in a house with too many rooms; the shop at dawn, first customer, first tea. Shoot all three. Long lens, backed off, keep rolling after each beat ends.

### What NOT to do with her

- **No VO lands on her raw lines. Ever.** "It would kill the taro" got twelve seconds; here the answer is zero. Her metaphors already beat your thesis-language.
- **No giggle exit from her hardest moment.** Ten confirmed charm-exits across three cuts. She will offer you the exit herself, because she laughs when it hurts. Decline it. Ask the smile question instead.
- **Do not narrate her interior.** No "I can feel a bitterness" moves. If you want to know what she feels, ask her, on camera.
- **Do not location-change off a wound.** If she breaks the surface, cancel whatever is next. Stay. If the crew offers tea after a heavy answer, wave it off for two more minutes.
- **Do not interview her only once.** Return each Simikot night: D3, D4, D6. Exhaustion and familiarity produce the real telling, and D6, after the border, is when your questions will finally be worthy of her. Re-ask question 12 on D6 and cut the two answers against each other.
- **Do not solve your own problems past her.** If the store is busy, a truck arrives, the power dies: film her handling it before you handle anything.

## D. THE OTHER WOMEN, THE NUNS, THE BORDER

### The woman at 1:03 (Simikot, D4)

She had many children and everyone left. Strong presence. Her structural job: the counterweight, and the control case. She is Jaba without the store. What does staying look like without the road's one blessing, the shop income? Your sharpest gap in three cuts was that everyone on camera is gung ho and nobody has given up (M5). Do not force her into that slot, but leave the door open. Jaba and this woman can be your Due and Meadu, the two-sided structure the Nile cut already proved you can hold.

1. How many children did you raise in this house?
2. Where is each of them now? *(Let her go through them one by one. Do not rush the list. The list is the scene.)*
3. Who left first? How did they go, on foot or by road?
4. What do they send home? What do you send them?
5. Who will harvest with you this year? Who do you eat with?
6. When the road came, what did you hope it would bring?
7. What did it bring?
8. Do you think any of them will come back to live? *(If she says no, or shrugs: the shrug is the shot. Ten seconds. Then:)* When did you stop expecting it?
9. If the road had never come, would your children still be here? *(Her honest answer might be "they'd have walked out anyway." That complicates your thesis and you want it.)*
10. What is this village like during Dashain, and what is it like the week after?

If she has given up: do not cheer her up on camera. Put her resignation adjacent to Jaba's endurance in the edit.

### The apple farmer (near Okhren/Simikot, D5 morning)

Structural job: the blessing, embodied. The one person for whom the road is unambiguous money. The work is the scene; ask while the hands keep working. Pick alongside them before the real questions.

1. Show me a good apple. What makes it good?
2. How many trees? Who planted them? *(If a parent planted them, follow that.)*
3. Before the road, what happened to this fruit? *(The honest answer: it fell, the wasps got it, price zero. Get them to say it.)*
4. What does a crate fetch here, at the tree? What does it fetch in Kathmandu? Who takes the difference?
5. What happens to the harvest if the road closes for a week? *(The landslide question. The whole fragility theme in one practical answer.)*
6. What happened the year the border closed and the pilgrims stopped coming?
7. Who picks these apples? Where are the young ones? *(If the answer is women and old men, say nothing, film the hands. Loops the apples back to the women's story.)*
8. Is anyone planting new trees because of the road? Are you?
9. Do your children want this orchard? *(The clock question. An orchard is a 20-year bet on people staying.)*

### The nuns at Yalbang (D5, around the 3pm ritual; Tibetan spoken)

Structural job: women who chose a different answer to the same mountain. Everywhere else on this road, women stay because men leave. The nunnery is a community of women who stay on purpose, together. That inversion is the scene's whole point. Do not shoot it as exotic ritual b-roll, and do not say the frame to them; ask the questions that let them say their version.

1. What time did your day start? Walk me through it to now.
2. How did you come to this monastery? How old were you? What did your family say? *(Gently. Some were sent as children. Either answer matters.)*
3. Do you have brothers? Where are they? *(Odds are high the answer is Taklakot or India. The nun whose brothers crossed the border is the theme inside the monastery walls.)*
4. What has the road changed for the monastery? What arrives now that didn't?
5. Where is life better for a woman, here or in a village below? *(Plain words, one comparison. Either answer is a headline.)*
6. Since the road came, do more girls arrive here, or fewer? If the villages below empty, what happens to this monastery? *(Their recruitment is a road story too.)*
7. What is here that did not come up the road? What is here that the road can never take away?
8. What do you pray for, for the people on the road? *(Buses crash on these corridors. If she prays for travelers, you have the mountain's danger stated as devotion, better than any statistic.)*
9. There is climate monitoring equipment here. Who checks it? What is the mountain doing that it didn't used to do? *(Only if it shows on camera, M10.)*

Scene intentions: the ritual complete and unnarrated; one nun at work, not at prayer (cooking, carrying water, tending the instruments); one held wide of the nunnery above the road, trucks passing tiny below. No VO on it.

### Hilsa (D6): who is the road actually for

The question comes from your own audience. Vivek, verbatim: "Who is the road actually for? Billions in trade move through Rasuwa, but Rasuwa stays poor." Ask it at the one place it answers itself, out loud, at the rail of the bridge, as field restatement #3. Then let three people answer it without knowing they're answering it.

**A returning worker** (crossing back from Taklakot with the one permitted sack):
1. What's in the sack?
2. How long were you gone? What did you do there? What did you earn in a day? *(Around 300 yuan, near 6,000 rupees. Let him say the number.)*
3. Who is waiting for you at home? Who kept your fields while you were gone?
4. Why Taklakot and not Kathmandu? *(The honest answer: the border is closer than the capital. That sentence is the geopolitics of your episode, delivered by a man with a sack.)*
5. Will you go back next season? Will your son?

**A lodge owner or shopkeeper in Hilsa:**
1. Where does everything in this building come from?
2. What happens here when China takes a holiday? *(In May, imports stopped for five days because Taklakot's warehouses closed for May Day. Ask if she remembers it.)*
3. The border was shut for five seasons *(essay research; let her give the number)*. What happened to this town? Who decided to close it? Did anyone ask you?
4. Fifteen helicopter rotations a day in season. What do the pilgrims buy from you? What do they see of this place?
5. When did you last go to Kathmandu? What did the trip take? Is Nepal far from here? *(Ask exactly that. The lodge owner in the essay said her complaint was not that China was too close but that Nepal was too far. Give it space to arrive on its own.)*

**A trader or truck driver coming down:** What is in the truck? Who owns the cargo? Who owns the truck? Does any of it stop in Humla, or does Humla just watch it pass? And the closer, for anyone with time to think, through the translator: "Kathmandu says this road is important for the country. Important for whom?"

**A pilgrim, if approachable:** one question only. What did you see from the helicopter? *(Their answer, mountains, nothing, against everything you have spent five days inside, is a cut, not a lecture.)*

## E. CRAFT GUARDRAILS

- **One word: SEPARATION.** If a scene is about infrastructure and not separation, it is an explainer beat: keep it short and elite, or cut it.
- **One face: Jaba.** An issue without a face does not travel. Every macro thread (China, remittances, landslides) must physically route through her store, her shelves, her family, or the road between her and her son. If a thread cannot reach her counter, it goes in a different episode. The other women exist to triangulate her. Protect her screen time against the corridor's spectacle. Ration the drone.
- **Legible stakes: she can visibly win or lose.** Win: store thrives, sons come for Dashain, apples sell. Lose: road closes, shelves empty, nobody comes home. Shoot both poles, and name her stake on camera by end of D4.
- **The clocks, on screen and dated:** crates, road status, your 8-day passage, Dashain, winter.
- **Nightly test, out loud, to a crew member:** "Full shelves, empty house." If today's footage didn't feed that sentence, tomorrow's must.
- **Free Solo applies to the corridor.** Pre-teach the danger once, early (single lane, cliff edge, night ban, the Sep 20 slide), so when the road performs, the scene plays wordless.
- **Honeyland applies to Simikot and Yalbang.** Small crew, natural light, held silence, body language first. Would the inventory walk, the empty rooms, the dawn shutters read with the sound off? Spend the Simikot hours ON her, not on coverage of the town.
- **The hybrid:** open with the Harris engine, anchor in minute one, re-hook at minute three, then hand the arc to Jaba the way Foster's telling carries Octopus Teacher. The vérité payoffs (the message, the empty rooms, the dawn shop) are scenes the curiosity loop has earned, not ambient texture. Shoot Honeyland, structure it like Free Solo, thread it like Bourdain, package it like Borders.
- **Ending law:** no homework ending, no tradeoff VO in maximum fry. End on transformation and a face. Octopus Teacher kills the octopus and still comforts; loss and lift at once. Jaba already does both. That is what the smile is.
- **Two sins to stay clean of:** compress reality, never invent it (no relocated footage, no spliced meaning; defend every cut to Jaba's face), and don't let the formula telegraph itself. These beats are scaffolding, not a template the audience should feel.

*Sixty seconds, every morning, before the jeep.*
