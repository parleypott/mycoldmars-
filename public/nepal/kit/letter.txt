# A Letter Before the Road
*from a teacher, the night before you drive*

Dear Johnny,

By the time you hear this you will be somewhere on the worst good road in the world, and I want you to know before anything else that I think you are exactly where you should be, on that road and in your work. You are standing at the particular threshold every documentary maker I have loved eventually reaches, the place where being good at explaining stops being enough, and something quieter and harder starts calling. You have heard it calling. I have read what you have been asking yourself lately, about presence, about feeling your way into a story instead of narrating your way over it. You have enough checklists already. So this is me sitting you down the night before, the way somebody once sat me down, to tell you the one thing I know. Then I will walk the road with you in my head, day by day, and show you where the one thing will be waiting.

Here is the one thing. You already find the moments that matter. I have watched three of your rough cuts now, closely, more closely than is polite, and the moments are all there. A woman on the Nile told you her village was gone and said it in five words: it's over, it's the dam. Someone in Palau told you what the rising water would do in five more: it would kill the taro. And in Myanmar you shot the strongest ending of your career, your friend Jack's face in a hot-air balloon, eyes closed, five years of fear briefly setting down its luggage. You found all of it. Your eye has never been the problem. The problem is what happens next. Two seconds after the dam line, you were speaking again. Your narration landed on the taro twelve seconds after the words. Over Jack's face you hung a card promising a conclusion the moment had already delivered. And whenever a laugh was offered at somebody's hardest moment, you took it, because taking it felt kind. You keep opening doors and then closing them gently before anyone has to walk through.

I understand why. I want you to know that I understand why, because this habit of yours is a virtue misapplied. You are a kind man. When a person in front of you touches their own grief, your instinct is to rescue them from the silence, and when your audience might be confused, your instinct is to help. Both instincts made you great at explanation. But you have set your sights on something past explanation now, and at that level the kindest thing you can do for a person is to stay. Hold the camera steady, hold your tongue, and let the moment finish what it started in them. The audience, meanwhile, is waiting for your permission to feel, and silence, on camera, is that permission.

So this trip has one discipline in it, underneath all the particulars I am about to give you. When something true happens, count ten seconds before you speak. Ten full seconds. It will feel like standing in a doorway in winter. Stand there anyway. And when you do finally speak, do not summarize, and do not comfort. Ask the smaller question. Not how does that feel, never how does that feel. Ask what she cooked the morning her son left. Ask where she was standing when the first truck came. Grief lives in kitchens and doorways, and people can walk toward it one object at a time when they cannot walk toward it any other way.

Now let me tell you about the woman you are driving toward, because she is the reason I believe this episode can be the one where you cross over.

Her name is Jaba, and you have already seen her, in the scout footage, sitting in her shop in Simikot in a red cardigan with shelves of Coca-Cola and biscuits rising behind her like a wall. Watch that clip again on the plane. She gives you the entire theme of your film in two consecutive breaths, without being asked. It's much easier now with roads, she says. And then: our kids are not here, our family is separated. There is your whole story, the gift and the theft in one mouth, and she said it unprompted, to a camera she had only just met. You do not need to go find a thesis in Nepal. You need to sit with the woman who already carries it, and go deeper than the sentence she has learned to say.

And carry a sentence of your own in your pocket, the one a viewer should be able to say to a friend the next morning: a new road finally reached the last roadless district in Nepal, and it filled a woman's shelves with everything except her family. Four words, if you need them shorter: full shelves, empty house. Every scene you shoot either proves that sentence, complicates it, or belongs to a different film. And notice what the sentence is actually about. Not roads. Not development. Separation. Every person who will ever watch this film has someone far away because some road of their own carried them off toward work, and that is the string you are plucking. The road is your instrument. Separation is your song.

Go back to the clip once more, because the harder thing to notice is what her face is doing. She smiles when she says the family is separated. She may even laugh. And I need you to understand what that smile is, because everything depends on it. It is a door held politely shut. People who have carried something a long time learn to hand it to strangers wrapped, so nobody has to be embarrassed. Your habit, the one we just talked about, is to accept the wrapped version and move on. In Palau a homeowner told you there was no choice, her family would have to move, and she was smiling as she said it, and the smile went unquestioned, and the scene ended, and whatever the smile was holding never made it into the film. Do not let that happen in Simikot. When Jaba smiles over the wound, that is the invitation. Wait your ten seconds, and then say, gently, the truest thing in the room: you are smiling while you tell me this. Why? And then do not say another word for as long as it takes. I promise you that what happens in the next thirty seconds will be worth more than every drone shot in the Himalaya.

There is one more thing about Jaba you must know before you arrive. In the scout footage her husband is standing beside her behind the counter. He has since left, like her sons before him, gone down the road for work. She is alone now. Which means the archive is holding a gift for you, the kind an editor prays for. Two frames, the same shop, the same counter, the same woman. In one of them a man stands beside her. In the other, air. When you get there, shoot her exactly where she stood in the scout clip. Do not explain the composition to anyone, just make it. And then, if the day has earned enough trust, show her the old footage on your phone and film her watching it, and ask her only this: what do you see? Whatever she does next, you know the discipline. Ten seconds. The smaller question.

And because her youngest son works in Surkhet, the town where your journey begins, the road itself has laid a scene at your feet. Before you drive north, have your fixer call ahead and ask two permissions, hers and the boy's, for something simple. An hour with him. Film his hands doing his work. Then ask him to record a short message to his mother on your phone, whatever he wants to say. Then carry it up the road for three days, the same road that carried him away, and in her shop, with her permission, hold the phone up and press play. You and the road will have done the same job that week, ferrying pieces of a family back and forth. If either of them hesitates, at any point, drop the whole idea without a flicker of disappointment, because a moment like this is only worth having if it is freely given. But if it happens, hold her face and do not make a sound. You will be tempted, afterward, to say something about distance and love. The moment will already have said it. Anything you add will only prove you were not fully there.

While you are in her world, make two more scenes with her, and make them slowly. The first is a walk along her own shelves. Ask her to pick up five things, one at a time, and for each one tell you what it costs now, what it cost before the road, and how it used to get to Simikot. Somewhere in the third or fourth object she will say the word backs, or legs, or days, because everything on those shelves used to arrive on a human body, sometimes hers, and when she says it, you have the entire economics of the film in a woman's hands, no graphics required. Shoot it twice, once with her talking, once silent and close, just hands and cans and light.

The second scene is her house, if she offers it. Whose bed was whose. Which room went quiet first. Shoot only what she shows you, close and in daylight, and if she stops talking somewhere in a doorway, you know by now that the quiet is the scene. One lens lesson you paid for in Palau belongs in this house with you: a ranger once pointed his flashlight at the single tree holding back the ocean, the best image in your whole cut, and the audience could not see it, a green smudge in the dark. When somebody shows you the small thing everything depends on, get the close shot, in daylight, before you leave. The heartbreaking object deserves the same care as the beautiful one.

A second woman in Simikot deserves a visit of her own. Your notes already flagged her, and shoots have a way of eating intentions, so consider this the second flag. In the scout footage there is a woman who told you she had many children and every one of them left. That is Jaba's story at a later chapter, an answer to the question of what the coming years hold. Go back to her. Ask her the question Jaba cannot answer yet: what is it like after the last one goes? Two women, same wound, different depths. Your film needs both soundings.

Now, the road itself. You will be on it for two days before you ever reach her, and I want you to drive it differently than you drove Myanmar and the Nile. Here is the pattern I need you to break, and I will say it plainly because I love you and plain is what teachers owe their students. In your last three films, every time reality tried to hand you tension, a fuel line or a checkpoint or a closed road, you solved your own problem as efficiently as possible and filmed the relief. The relief is real and human and I like you in those moments. But a story about hardship where the storyteller never stays inside the hardship is only ever a rumor of hardship. Your viewers were told this place is difficult. They watched it be easy for you.

So on this drive, the rule is simple. The obstacle is the scene. The road will be blocked. A landslide closed it the very week you left. When it happens, the delay is the footage. Get out of the car. Find the truck driver who has been sitting there since yesterday and learn his name and what he is carrying and what it costs him to sit still. If there is a queue, walk to the back of it before your crew works its way to the front. The man at the back has waited the longest and has the least reason to perform. And when someone tells you to put the camera away, put the big camera away and keep a phone running inside the car, pointed at your own worried face if nothing else, because the whispered argument about whether to keep going is precisely the scene your last three films are missing. The fear you feel at a checkpoint is the only proof your audience will ever get that the stakes are real.

While you drive, tell people what you are looking for. I mean this literally, on camera, at every stage of the journey. Say to a driver, to a farmer, to whoever is beside you: everyone tells me this road is the best thing that ever happened here, so why does every village I pass seem to have no young men in it? You have a habit of stating your question beautifully in the first two minutes of a film and then not saying it again for half an hour, and somewhere in there the audience quietly forgets what the journey is for. It costs you nothing to keep asking it out loud, to real people, and their answers will become the film's spine, without a single line of narration doing the work.

Two more habits of the road, while we are still in the jeep. The first is about the people you are traveling with. In Myanmar you brought a friend who knew the country and barely asked him a question on camera, and your friend Robert, watching the cut, noticed, and asked why. On the Nile you introduced a producer who partly grew up in Egypt and she never spoke again. Meanwhile the one companion you did lean on, your guide Jack, quietly became the emotional spine of the whole Myanmar cut, the only person whose story completes. The lesson is sitting right there. Whoever rides beside you in that jeep, your fixer, your producer, turn to him at the strange moments and ask, on camera: what am I looking at? Have you seen this before? What would your mother say about this place? Either use the person beside you or leave them out of the frame, but do not do the half thing again, where the audience meets a guide and never hears what he knows.

The second habit is about promises. You have a rule you have already proven on the Nile, where you raised a dam early, told the audience plainly that its meaning would come later, and paid it off forty minutes downstream, and it worked beautifully. And you have the opposite habit too, mentioning a civil war in one clause and never showing a frame of it, which leaves a hole shaped exactly like the thing you named. So make it a discipline this time: if you speak a danger or a wonder aloud, either show it, or openly promise it for later and keep the promise, or cut the mention entirely. An audience forgives you for not going everywhere. It does not forgive being told about a wolf it never sees.

A few smaller things, offered with a lighter hand.

When you eat, ask about the food. Really ask. How it is made, what it cost before the road came, whether the chilies rode up on a truck. You skipped those questions at dinners on the Nile and in Palau, and both times you left depth sitting on the table. Food is the one subject where curiosity never reads as intrusion. The same goes for any song you hear. Ask where it comes from. An old woman singing something her mother taught her, and telling you what it meant on the trail in the years before the road, is worth twenty minutes of anyone explaining anything.

When you reach the nunnery at Yalbang, farther up the road, where the trucks pass tiny below the walls, remember that you are meeting women who answered the same mountain differently than Jaba did. Do not treat them as texture. Ask one nun the real question, the one this whole film is secretly about: everyone else's children are leaving, and you stayed, and you chose these walls. What do you know that the road does not? And find, somewhere along the way, one person who has simply given up, who thinks nothing can be fixed and says so. Robert, watching your Palau cut, noticed that every person in it was still fighting the good fight, that nobody on screen had surrendered, and he was right to flag it. Hope on screen is only worth something when the audience has met its alternative.

At the far end of the road, where a bridge crosses the river into China, remember the question a viewer in Kathmandu sent you about another border road, in another district that stayed poor while billions in trade rolled through it: who is the road actually for? It is better than anything I could write. Stand on the bridge and ask it out loud, then look around and count how much of what crosses ever stops in Humla, the district you will be standing in, the last one in Nepal a road ever reached.

And hold on to the ending your producers have already planned, because it is better than it looks on the schedule. On your last day, in a Kathmandu market, you are meant to find apples from Karnali being sold, apples that rode the whole road you just drove. Do that scene with everything you have. Ask the vendor what a Karnali apple sells for and what it sold for when it came by airplane, and listen for the truth hiding in the price: an apple's cost was never in the apple, it was in the road. And then let the bitter rhyme land where only you can hear it, that the first cargo this road ever carried away for money was the men. Whether you say that out loud in the film or let the images say it, decide months from now. But when you are cutting this thing, remember Jack in the balloon, and how close you came to burying the best ending of your career under a card that promised more talking. Do not write the conclusion this time. Shoot an ending shaped like a person, Jaba's face if she gives it to you, and in the edit, try black before you try words. You may find the film has already ended, a shot before you were planning to end it.

I want to say one last thing about the bigger ambition, the one you sometimes seem almost embarrassed to say out loud: that you want millions of people, not a festival audience, and you fear the craft I am describing belongs to the small room. It does not. The most watched documentaries of your lifetime are, almost without exception, films about one person you come to love, filmed with the patience I am asking of you, structured with the momentum you already know how to build. An old man making sushi. A diver befriending an octopus. People watched those because the filmmaker stayed in the room long enough for a stranger to become someone they loved, and then the leaving hurt. Your retention graphs and your instincts for momentum are the delivery system for that patience. You are carrying the small room's fire into the big one, and almost nobody on that platform is even attempting it, which is precisely the opening.

Since you have asked yourself where sophisticated craft actually loses a big audience, I will save you the study. The failures are fewer and more specific than you fear. Big audiences walk away from a documentary for four reasons. They never met a person to love. They could never tell what was at stake. The film ended like homework, informing them one last time instead of letting them feel what they came to feel. Or they could not retell it the next day at dinner, which is the quiet death, because word of mouth is the only marketing that matters at your scale. Notice what is not on that list. Patience is not on it. Silence is not on it. Ambiguity, even, is not on it, as long as the person and the stakes are clear. You have the person. You have the sentence. And you have something most patient films never get, a clock, three of them actually: the apples ripening toward harvest, a road that can close at any hour, and your own eight days running out. Let the audience feel all three ticking and you can be as patient as you like inside them.

So here is what I am asking of you, as clear as I can make it. On this trip, once a day, when something true is happening in front of you, I want you to notice the exact moment you feel the urge to speak, to help, to lighten, to move, and I want you to stay instead. Ten seconds. That is the entire assignment. Everything else, the shot lists and the schedules and the setpieces, matters far less than this one repeated act of staying. You have spent ten years learning to be interesting. This is the trip where you practice being present, on camera, with the discipline of a professional and the heart you have carried all along. The thing you have been looking for is not out there past the last high pass. It is in the ten seconds.

Go be with her. Let the mountain do its work on you, the way it has worked on everyone who ever walked in before the road, and bring back the film you have been circling for three countries.

With great affection, and greater expectation,

your teacher
