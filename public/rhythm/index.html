Red Rising is your twin. A low-caste miner infiltrates the ruling elite to bring down an extraction-colony caste system. Deep politics, near-zero felt difficulty, a runaway hit. It lives at a 3 to 4. That is your target. The one-line brief for the whole book: Red Rising, written by someone who actually knows how colonies work.

Here is the thing that should relax you. Dune and Red Rising carry the same amount of politics. The difference is not quantity. Dune makes you learn its politics; Red Rising makes you bleed them. Right now your canon is a magnificent Dune-shaped pile of systems, the physics of the doors, the four-generation proxy war, the client-patron economics, all of it real and all of it load-bearing. The entire job of the rhythm is to convert that pile into bodies. You do not have a complexity problem. You have a delivery problem, and delivery is solvable.

The one law

Five books, five different authors, and every report came back to the same sentence. This is the whole study compressed to one line:

Readers tolerate infinite complexity through a body they care about, and reject even a little of it as explanation.

And its operational form, the thing to keep taped above the desk:

The moment the reader is learning your world faster than they are feeling it, you have crossed from Red Rising into Dune. Pull back to the nearest wounded body and let the politics leak out of it.

Everything below is that law, applied to the exact machine of the training years.

Your three instincts, graded

You said three things in your message. Here is the verdict on each, because you were mostly right and the corrections are the whole craft.

1. "Everything should maybe be a scene. Exposition is lazy."

80% right Scene beats summary almost every time, and yes, reaching for exposition when you could do the hard yards of dialogue is usually laziness. But the other twenty percent is load-bearing, and it is not laziness, it is compression. The rule the craft actually runs on: dramatize the moment a value changes; summarize the interval that merely accumulates. A scene needs a turn, something flipping from safe to exposed, from loyal to suspicious, from a tool to a thing with its own ideas. The fortieth ledger lesson is summary. The lesson where a minister lets slip that the King is dying is a scene. If you can write a beat as one sentence of report and lose nothing, it was never a scene. So: not everything a scene. Roughly eighty percent scene, and the twenty percent of summary is the thing that lets months pass without the reader dying of real-time.

2. "Should he train and be the prince in the same chapter, or oscillate?"

braid, don't alternate Clean A/B/A/B alternation keeps your two threads in separate rooms, and that drains the exact friction you want. Your threads are not independent. The whole engine of the book is that court training is the cover and the fuel for the secret army. What he learns in the treasury tells him what to build. A minister's suspicion threatens the workshop. So braid them inside the chapter, with one dominant thread per chapter, and make the scene turn on the seam between the two lives, the moment court-knowledge solves a build-problem, or a build-problem nearly blows his cover at court. Reserve pure single-thread chapters for the two or three peaks where one thread needs a clear runway (the first time the army acts on its own; the day he is nearly caught).

3. "How much time passes per chapter? Clunk month-by-month, or leap?"

leap, on hinges Clunking month by month is boring; vanishing whole years is cheating. You avoid both by rendering results, not reps. Do not show the training montage (prose montage is twenty minutes of reading; film montage is ninety seconds, which is why training scenes work on screen and die on the page). Instead: open a chapter on a hard seasonal hinge ("By the second winter"), and let the reader fill the gap. End one scene with him fumbling a thing; open the next, months later, with him doing it cold, and the gap itself dramatizes the growth. Let objects carry the clock, the army goes from three units to thirty, his hands scar, a minister greys. And use the habitual past ("most mornings the smith-bot would...") to compress a hundred days into a rhythm, then break the rhythm on the day it goes wrong. The break is your scene.

How much geopolitics — the real answer

This is the one you actually asked, and it is your genuine edge, so I want to be precise. You are a geopolitics expert; the instinct is to get the whole system onto the page because you can see the whole system. Resist it. Your expertise should show up not as the clarity of your explanation but as the specificity of your transactions. The rule from every political hit, Red Rising to Six of Crows: dramatize the transaction, never the policy. Let the reader be the economist.

You never write "the regime taxes the road and strangles the colony." You show one cart stopped at one checkpoint, one bribe paid, one family's ore-count lighter this year than last, and the reader builds the system themselves from the instance. A tariff is abstract. A widow counting coins she does not have while a soldier waits is a scene. The economics research even named the single best one for you: the founding horror of Nicholas was not a massacre, it was the day he stopped buying the ore and started taxing it. Make that day a scene, one man realizing he has been robbed by an accounting change, and you have taught colonial extraction without a word of theory.

So here is the triage of your actual canon. Everything you have sorts into three bins.

Bin 1 — dramatize the transaction (make it a scene)

The tribute count at the Custom House. The day Nicholas started taxing instead of buying. The strike cautionary tale, told by someone still afraid of it. Mira's marriage as her wound, her anger at her father. The age-twelve mine visit. The red phone explained by the person who once had to use it. These are where your expertise becomes drama. Each is one body absorbing one move of the system.

Bin 2 — one vivid noun in passing (texture, never a paragraph)

The supernova that tore the door open. Osmarine holding the throat open. The seventy-one doors. The two moons conjoining every twenty years (never explained on the page — it is simply why the Vicentennial falls when it does). Vesh and Corr. State each once, as accepted history, the way a real person mentions the highway that built their town. Never stop to prove the physics.

Bin 3 — load-bearing but invisible (in your notes, holding the book up from underneath)

The full four-generation chronology of the Great Rupture. The complete vassal-world economics. The Chandrasekhar-limit mechanics. The Dune test: could this fact move to a glossary without the plot noticing? Then it is already in the glossary — make it a noun or cut it. The reader should be able to infer this bin and never be taught it. This is the difference between a book that feels deep and a book that feels like homework: the deepest layer is the one you can feel but never see.

The killer app: every lesson loads the army

This is the technique that solves your whole middle at once, and it comes straight out of how Rowling teaches an entire magical system through classes without a single lecture. Never explain a system except through a person who is grading, testing, or manipulating the prince with it. The archivist, the trade minister, the defense minister are not information delivery. They are rivals and skeptics and seducers, each with a bias and an agenda, and the geopolitics enters as their slant on it.

And here is the part that is pure BURGUNDY: in Harry Potter, every lesson is secretly Chekhov's gun being loaded while you think you are in class. Polyjuice is homework in book two and the infiltration engine by the climax. So map each of the prince's rotations to the exact thing the revolution will need, and the training stops being education and becomes an arsenal being assembled in plain sight.

The ArchivesThe old archivist, kind and quietly terrified of his own knowledge, shows Burgundy the maps and histories of the Great Rupture — and this is where, hunting through post-war survey maps, he finds the depot's location. The lesson loads: the target.
External AffairsMinister Schwartz teaches the vassal worlds — that they traded with each other before Nicholas severed the web. The lesson loads: why it matters, and the moral engine of the ending.
The Custom HouseThe trade minister walks him through the tribute machinery, the counts, the ore leaving. The lesson loads: the chokepoint — exactly what Burgundy denies Nicholas in the last line of the book.
DefenseThe defense minister, proud of the fortress, explains the weapons depot: pure corvite all around, fifty years to dig, impossible. The lesson loads: the heist, and the impossibility Burgundy alone can break.
Oz / the PreserveThe surveillance and informant apparatus, taught by the man who runs it. The lesson loads: the threat he must evade — the near-catches that pressurize the whole midsection.
The LoungeDuring training he learns the red phone, the escalation ladder, Shanti at the top. The lesson loads: the final beat — the call he will make over his father's body.

Six rotations, six scenes, each one a compelling hour of court drama that also quietly hands him a piece of the endgame. The reader thinks they are watching a boy learn to be a prince. They are watching him assemble a war.

The cadence model

Concretely, for the years seventeen to nineteen on the three-year clock to the Vicentennial. Roughly twelve to fourteen chapters, front-loaded, because year one is where everything is new and year three should compress hardest. One turn per scene, one change per chapter, one dated clock over all of it, and never a rep you can imply with a result.

What this asks you to decide

Four calls, and they are the ones that actually shape the writing. I will put these on the master questionnaire so they round-trip like everything else.

Decision 1 · the structure

Confirm the braided, twelve-to-fourteen-chapter midsection, one rotation per chapter, and the minister-to-army-need mapping above. This is my strong recommendation.

Decision 2 · the geopolitics dosage

Confirm the three-bin triage — that the Great Rupture chronology and the door physics stay in Bins 2 and 3 (a noun, or invisible), and only the transactions become scenes. This is the single biggest lever on whether you read like Red Rising or like Dune.

Decision 3 · the point of view (the quiet big one)

Hunger Games and Red Rising quarantine their politics with a tight POV — the past can only arrive as triggered memory or present need, never as narration. You are in close third, which can do the same job (Harry Potter is close third) but only with the discipline of it. The call: commit to strict POV quarantine — no fact reaches the reader unless a character on the page needs it now. Not a rewrite to first person; a rule we hold.

Decision 4 · the clock

Lock the single dated countdown that sits over the whole midsection (the Vicentennial at twenty, with the dig clock nested inside it). Every leap forward should also be a step toward that detonation. This ties to the dig-timing card already in the questionnaire.

The bottom line

You are chasing the exact thing Red Rising and Hunger Games proved you can have: real political weight carried at a mass-market pulse. The math is not "less geopolitics." It is same geopolitics, delivered through bodies instead of explanation. Your unfair advantage is that you actually know how a strangled colony smells and sounds and rations, and that lived texture is the thing mass readers reward and the thing a desk-bound worldbuilder can never fake. Spend your expertise there, on the checkpoint and the ore-count and the frightened archivist, and let the whole magnificent system hold the book up from underneath, where the reader feels it and never has to see it.
