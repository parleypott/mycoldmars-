BURGUNDY — The State of the Story

A full read of all twenty-eight chapters, measured against the thing you said you want: an emotional core from the beginning, people with real interiors, and drives the reader can feel. Written to be listened to. Settle in; this runs about half an hour.

Where the book actually is

I read the whole manuscript tonight, front to back, the way a reader would. Twenty-two chapters of real prose and six chapters of scaffolding at the end, the frames for the middle through the end of Act One. About forty-eight thousand words of finished writing, and another three and a half thousand of structured frames. I also re-read the canon documents, the house style, and every one of the hundred and eighty-seven questions that were sitting open on q5, and I sent a small army of readers through every chapter behind me to check my impressions against the text. While I was at it, I audited the questionnaire itself: thirty-four questions the manuscript had already answered are gone, forty were re-grounded against the current text, and five new ones now sit at the top. It stands at one hundred fifty-eight cards, every one of them still worth your time. So what follows is not a vibe. It's a measured read.

Here is the one-sentence version, and I want to say it plainly because you'll be driving: the front half of this book has crossed over from a clever premise into an actual novel, the emotional core you asked for is genuinely present in the first act and in the newest material, and the real work cut out for you is not fixing what exists. It's three things: giving your people bodies and faces, keeping Tuck alive through the whole book instead of a third of it, and writing the middle with the same emotional standard the edges now have. Everything else is cleanup and decisions.

Now let me earn that sentence.

What is working, and why it works

The opening two chapters are doing exactly what you rebuilt them to do. Chapter one gives Burgundy a want with a face on it before it gives him a single piece of worldbuilding. The shaking hand. The timer at thirty-one hours. The line that tells us everything about the stakes of the demonstration, which is not a line about mining at all: "Tomorrow morning his father would look at him longer than he had in years." That's the emotional engine of the entire book installed on page one, and it's installed in an object and a number instead of a speech. And then the lozenge. The boy at the chisel, hands grey to the wrist, the father slapping the candy out of his hand and crushing it under his heel. When Burgundy admits to himself, "I built it for a boy whose name I do not know," the book's two wants, to be seen by the father and to be worth something to the boy, are both live before the first chapter closes. That is an emotional core from the beginning. It's there. Protect it.

Chapter two is the best scene in the book and I think you know it. The King doctrine rewrite landed. What makes "I rule men" work is that the King never raises his voice and never stops being right on his own terms. The boot against the wheel, pushed "not hard, the way a man tests a cart's brake." The laugh that wounds Burgundy coming not from the King but from a nameless old man with ink on his collar, which is somehow so much worse. And the craft lines have real menace precisely because they're true: the foreman walks the seam because working men talk; a machine that digs quietly tells the King nothing. The scene is a rejection scene, but it's also a seduction scene, the first lesson in a craft the whole middle of the book then teaches. That double duty is what a strong spine looks like.

The style discipline is holding across the manuscript, and when it lands, it lands hard. Three beats I'd put in any pitch for this book. The kindest thing his father ever said to him, said to a coat. The Hollow chapter closing on the two flattest, best words in the book: "With arithmetic." And Essa's pillows, which is for my money the most quietly devastating passage you've written: Burgundy lies on a warm red velvet pillow all morning shooting at nothing, and only later does his mother tell him the seamstress died before the thaw with the King's one question, whether the pillows would be finished on time, hanging over her deathbed. The warmth of the pillow arrives in the reader's memory like a delayed charge. That is restraint doing emotional work that no amount of stated feeling could do. The book knows how to do this. The task is to do it more often, in more places.

The sensory world is an asset you should trust harder. The Bottom Market chapter is the most alive thing in the manuscript. The umberroots with their edges laced black, the two-finger spice pinch, the woman complaining about her son in slang while she fries, the scald ladled from a dented pot. And the machines of the poor, the one-motor fan-and-pump, the boy winding perfect coils one-handed while he eats, every one of them a small argument that nobody on that slope lacks the mind, only the parts and the time. That chapter does class, world, character, and theme simultaneously, without one sentence of explanation. And it contains the single best relationship beat in the front half: the scarr-pepper. Tuck saying "that one does end up in my kitchen, I mean your kitchen," the joke curdling, the awkward silence, the burn scar across his knuckles. Four years of invisible labor surfacing in one slip of a pronoun.

The turn of the book, the machines leaving, is genuinely thrilling. The logs read like a spark taking to dry grass, "it improved the improving," and then the walk down the beautiful tunnel into the dead kingdom and out into South 12 is the best sustained sequence you have. And it obeys the deepest structural rule without being told: it's the point of no return, the machines crossing beyond him is the door shutting behind the whole story, and the book treats it with that weight.

And then there's the Hollow. Chapter twenty-two is marked WIP, and it's already the strongest new material in the book. Hesta's table is where the political novel and the emotional novel finally sit down in the same room. The tally-book that only fills and never empties, pushed silently down the table. Skorn's line, "Easing the burden is what the festivals are for," which reclassifies Burgundy's entire life project in one quiet sentence. Hesta's wager speech about the strange kind of liar. And Wraven delivering the moral brief of the novel in under a minute: "They don't hang princes," then, "It's not like that for anyone else you met tonight," and then the whole thing in one small shrug: "So. Just, don't screw us over. That's all. That's the whole thing." When people ask what this book is about, that scene is the answer. Whatever else happens in the back half, that table has to stay its center of gravity.

The emotional-core audit, chapter by chapter

You asked the specific question, so I measured the specific thing. Where does the book make the reader feel with Burgundy, rather than watch him learn?

The first act scores high. One: the want and the shake. Two: the boot and the laugh. Three and four: the mother's reveal, which works because it keeps re-personalizing, she was seventeen too, she was priced into the marriage, she asked for the library the way a prisoner asks for a window. Five through seven: the Tuck flashback, the market, Ran's, all warm, all alive, carried by the friendship. The quilt of another boy's clothes at the foot of the bed might be the single best emblem in the book, two boys sewn together across a class line, year by year, by women who loved them both.

Then the middle arrives and the temperature drops. Chapters eight through eleven run mostly on curiosity and competence: the tribute mechanics, the council listening, the ledger world, the claw learning to hold a cup. It's good material, the eight-percent story is chilling and the cup-that-didn't-break is a lovely engineering beat, but for four chapters Burgundy mostly absorbs information and builds. His stated inner life thins to observation. And there's a specific pattern inside that drop worth naming, because once you see it you can fix it anywhere: the flat stretches are the ones where Burgundy is alone. The claw chapter, the osmarine chapter, the long walk down the tunnel, all of them are one man and his machines, no dialogue, nobody to want anything from. The book actually knows this about itself. The loneliest and best line in that whole stretch is the DREKKO swinging its lens toward his voice, "waiting for something it could use," which quietly names what a machine can't give him, and what his family gives him too. But the loneliness is mostly unwitnessed. It needs a witness, or a cost, every few chapters, or it reads as mood instead of wound. The feeling comes back with a vengeance at the hunt, and I want to flag the hunt as the model for how to fix the middle, because what changes there is that Burgundy stops witnessing and starts costing. He lies on a dead woman's needlework shooting deliberately wide. Oz's recruiting pitch happens while dead animals cool in the grass. And then the one syllable, "No," coming out with an edge that surprises everyone including him, is the first time in five chapters his want breaks the surface in public. The library confrontation that follows, mud on his shoes, his voice cracking at his mother, her answer arriving via Essa instead of via defense, is the emotional peak of the middle. Notice the pattern: the strong chapters are the ones where he wants something from a person in the room and doesn't fully get it. The flat stretches are the ones where he wants information and gets it.

The back of the finished material recovers strongly. Ran's arrest lands because the book lets Burgundy be guilty, not sad: "He collected the cores because of me," and Tuck, beautifully, "did not tell him it was not his fault. He had never been in the business of comfortable lies." Burgundy crying into the pillow is the first time the book lets him cry, and it's earned, and it's for someone else, which is exactly right. The girl saying "It doesn't get tired" gives his whole obsession back to him in four words from a stranger. And the Hollow, as I said, is the summit.

So the honest audit is this: the emotional core exists at the beginning, sags through the learning chapters, and returns whenever a relationship carries the load. The canon documents already diagnosed this, the yearning-to-be-seen thread, the being an object in both parents' machines. But right now that diagnosis lives more in the planning documents than on the page. The middle chapters need two or three beats where the reader watches the loneliness cost him something specific: a birthday square he didn't cut, which you already have and which is quietly perfect, the kitchen party heard through a wall, which you also have. More of those. They're cheap, they're short, and they're what keeps the reader's hand on his shoulder through the machinery.

The people, one at a time

Burgundy is the most complete person in the book, and he has one strange property: he has no body. Twenty-eight chapters and the reader could not pick him out of a lineup. No hair, no eyes, no skin, no height. The book actually depends on his face twice: Ran recognizes him off the King's young face stamped on a coin, and every trip below the wall requires hiding that face, and we've never seen it. You've now given me the seed, chocolate-brown hair, brown eyes, olive skin, tall but not Tuck-tall, and I've turned it into a proposal that's waiting for you at the top of q5. The short version: the coin already tells us he has his father's features, so let him have his father's face under his mother's coloring. The jaw strangers stop at, and the coloring no fourth-generation clansman on Dross has. His face becomes the emotional core made visible, everyone who looks at him sees someone else, the city sees the coin, the father sees the heir, and only Mira sees her family looking back. It costs two lines of prose and it makes his appearance mean something instead of just existing. There's a second question stacked under it, whether olive skin reads as off-world blood to a sharp eye on Dross, which has real consequences for every disguise scene and for how fast Hesta's people distrust him. Both cards are on q5 waiting for your ruling.

Tuck is the book's best-loved person and its most underused asset. Here is the arithmetic that surprised me: he is genuinely on the page in five chapters of twenty-eight. He owns the flashback, the market, and the first trip to Ran's, he gets the reunion at the cold storage and the corridor scene the night Ran is taken, and then he effectively vanishes. Between chapters eight and fifteen he exists as memory. After eighteen he is simply gone: the frames for the back half never mention him once, and they hand his most natural role, the food run to the Hollow, to a brand-new under-cook named Pell. Your own canon notes from early August say the opposite, that Tuck is the supply line and the food conversation is a running thread between the two of them. That contradiction between the canon and the frames is exactly the kind of thing that silently becomes a book, and it's flagged on q5 now. And this is the friendship the book's loneliness is measured against. When he is on the page, he outshines everyone: the ghost nickname, "who would I tell?", the barley-liquor scheme, the birthday cloth squares pressed into hands in corridors. Your instinct to invest in him is exactly right, and it's why I built the Tuck cards the way I did. The straw-white hair on the kid, planted in the flashback where his mother is already doing the two-boys-one-birthday routine. The grown version at the cold storage, still tall, still lanky, the straw gone dirty blonde, cut short and utilitarian, and the infectious smile paid off by the big laugh he's already laughing two lines later. And a third card that goes past appearance, because what you dictated about him is really a personality doctrine: the unguarded joy, always joking, always exaggerating, always hunting the amusement in things. The manuscript already writes him this way when it remembers to. The card proposes we canonize it, audit every Tuck line against it, and, the one craft decision inside it, let his joke-engine stall exactly once, the night Ran is taken, so the reader learns the jokes were load-bearing. And then never again, jokes thinner and braver in danger, all the way down. If you accept those three cards, Tuck stops being a warm memory the book visits and becomes its second pole.

Mira is magnificent for four chapters and then becomes infrastructure. The reveal chapters are among the best in the book because she keeps turning out to be a person: priced into a marriage at seventeen, locked in her wing for five days over cough medicine, telling a small boy the same bedtime story about machines coming down out of the sky, "rescuing us," until the boy became the story. That last beat, "You are my answer," is enormous. Two craft notes on her, though, both fixable. In the wing-reveal chapters the feeling lives mostly in the furniture, the unwalked carpet, the dust-angled chairs, the smell of books replacing wet stone, while Burgundy registers the geography with precision and registers almost nothing about the fact that his mother is finally looking at his secret work. The setting is carrying emotion the people should be trading. And in the library confession, her nineteen years of quiet interventions arrive as a run of list-paragraphs, "and remember your toy room, and your education, and at night," summary stacked on summary right at the moment the scene should slow down and let one of those memories play. Then, after the shipment chapter, she recedes into logistics, manifests, channels, and the canon's sharpest idea about her, that Burgundy slowly realizes he is also an object in her machine, her academy-of-one, her weapon against the regime that caged her, has no scene yet. It's in the frames, the surprise on the stairs when she doesn't come look, the broker chapter where her anger is a damage assessment. When you draft those, that's where her second act lives, and the book should let Burgundy feel the horror of being loved as an instrument by both parents at once. That feeling is the deepest thing this story has to offer, and it is one scene away from existing.

The King has a doctrine but needs a heartbeat. The presumptuous-not-temperamental rebuild made him genuinely frightening, and the craft lessons give him a perverse generosity, he is, in his way, the most honest teacher Burgundy has. But look at the ledger the book is building toward: a son who kills his father. For that to cost the reader something, the father has to be worth something, and right now the warm pan of the scale holds exactly one beat, "You're tired," the kindest thing, said to a coat. That beat works because it's almost warmth, seen through glass. The book needs two or three more like it, moments where the reader glimpses the man the King might have been, a hand on the shoulder that lingers one second past instruction, pride that slips out unperformed before he catches it. Not softening. Complication. The Long Drop monologue actually helps here, a man who mourns a lost theater of cruelty is at least a man with an interior. The audit found the older chapters still carry a couple of "seethed" and "furious" beats that contradict the doctrine, those are on q5 to sand down.

Hesta's table is the best ensemble work in the book and it arrived nearly whole. Hesta pricing everything, running the wager, never visibly thawing, "she just starts assigning him things, which from her is intimacy," that line in your frame is exactly right, keep it. Skorn's silence and his book of names. Tobin's warmth and the notched ear nobody asks about. Wraven, fourteen, milk-glass eye, the most surprising thing they have. Maren counting the turnings in a dead language. My only note here is protective: as the middle industrializes, the frames keep calling the group "an operation," and the temperature drop is intentional, but don't let the Hollow lose its dinners entirely. The suppers are what made them a group instead of an operation, your own frame says so, and the reader will need those dinners as much as the characters do.

The state of the text itself

Small but real: the manuscript is carrying visible battle damage from the editing campaigns, and it's concentrated in exactly the chapters you care most about right now. The cold-storage scene has a duplicated "You too Tuck," and later in the same chapter, at Ran's shop, the "I know her name" run around Essa is quadrupled: doubled inside Burgundy's speech and doubled again in a stray orphan paragraph that shouldn't exist at all. Chapter seventeen has two big duplicated blocks, each stamped four times, the osmarine cage description among them. And there are stutter artifacts scattered through the late middle chapters, "weekweekweekweeks," "HisHisHisHis," "ivoryyyy," a duplicated "Three villages, this was" at the hunt, and one sentence of editorial instruction that leaked into the prose near Tobin's handshake. None of this is craft, all of it is cleanup, and most of it was already ticketed on q5. I flag it because the cold-storage scene is where your new Tuck description lands, and we should heal that passage in the same pass that beautifies it.

The work cut out for you, ranked

If I stack everything this read surfaced, it sorts into five piles, biggest payoff first.

First, the people work, which is why we're here tonight. Faces for Tuck and Burgundy, the personality doctrine, and then the same treatment, one card's worth of decision each, for Mira, the King, and Hesta down the line. This is cheap in words and huge in effect. The five cards are live at the top of q5 right now.

Second, Tuck's continuity. The reunion currently reads as an errand, he gets Burgundy to Ran's, he passes messages, and the frames use him as a food channel. The friendship needs at least one scene per act that exists only because they like each other. The bread-run idea in your canon notes is the right instinct, Tuck supplying the Hollow feasts puts him back in the story's bloodstream, and the frames should be corrected to give that role back to him instead of Pell, or to make Pell his runner rather than his replacement. The decision you have to make, and it's on q5, is whether Tuck ever learns what's actually happening below. Right now Burgundy keeps him out on purpose, "The less you know, the less danger you're in," which is loving, but it means the book's warmest relationship is running on an omission, and the moment that omission surfaces, and it should, is a scene with real teeth in it.

Third, the middle's emotional spine. The training rotations in the new canon are the right machine for the worldbuilding, and the fix for the temperature is already proven inside your own manuscript: give the observer a want and a person in the way, the hunt method. Every rotation chapter should cost him something personal, not just teach him something political. And the being-seen thread needs its two or three planted beats, the uncut birthday square is the template.

Fourth, the back half. The frames are good, genuinely, the Hollow dinners, the armory and the proving range, the militarization vote with Hesta's "the day you build the second one." And the depot heist, the spine of the climax, isn't in the frames at all yet; it lives only in the canon documents. But they're frames. The real work cut out for you, in raw hours, is here: six framed chapters of prose at the standard the front half now sets, plus finishing the Hollow, and the drafting is blocked less by craft than by the open big calls on q5, the climax path, the clan name, the moon mechanics, the generations count. Every week those stay open, the back half stays theoretical.

Fifth, the sweep. Dedup the damaged passages, sand the King's two temper beats, finish the parked slots. Mechanical, ticketed, satisfying.

What I'd rule first

If you only have one q5 session in you this week, rule in this order. The five appearance cards at the top, they unlock immediate prose work I can execute the same day. Then the climax-path card, because it unblocks the entire back half. Then the naming cluster, clan, space-doors, red phone, because names are load-bearing across the six framed chapters. Then Tuck's back-half role. Everything after that is ordinary traffic.

Closing

You asked whether the story has an emotional core, and the fair answer after a full read is: it has one, it beats strongest at the beginning and at Hesta's table, and it goes quiet in the corridors between. The people are real wherever the book slows down long enough to let them want something from each other. The machinery, the tribute, the doctrine, the tunnels, the loop, is the best-built part of the manuscript, and it is no longer the point; it's the pressure vessel the feelings live inside. The boy who built a machine so his father would look at him longer than he had in years is a boy readers will follow all the way down that shaft. Give him a face, keep his friend beside him, make the father worth grieving, and let the mother's love be as complicated as her cargo. That's the book. It's closer than you think.

The manuscript sleeps at twenty-eight chapters. The questionnaire is awake at the top of q5. And I'll be here in the morning.
