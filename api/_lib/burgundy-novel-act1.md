# Puppy Town

## Act I: The Rico Uprising

*A novel in seven chapters*

---

## Chapter One — The Basement

Burgundy had been awake for thirty-one hours.

He could tell by the count blinking in the corner of his monitor — a small script he'd written to keep himself honest, ticking up the hours since he'd last slept. The number didn't bother him. The shake in his paws did, but only because it made the soldering hard.

The basement smelled like ozone and old copper. Three monitors glowed green-on-black in front of him, lines of code scrolling so fast they made the dim room flicker. Cables ran along the floor in bundles thicker than his arm, taped down with whatever he'd had on hand — duct tape, electrical tape, in one place a strip of his own shirt. Against the far wall, beneath a tarp, the prototype waited.

He hadn't looked at it in six hours. That was on purpose. If he looked he'd find something to fix, and he didn't have time to fix anything else. Tomorrow was the day.

The stairs creaked.

He didn't turn. Only one puppy came down those stairs at this hour.

"You should sleep," Tobin said.

"I'll sleep tomorrow."

"You said that yesterday."

"Then I'll sleep the day after tomorrow."

Tobin came around the workbench and dropped a bundle on it — black bread and something that smelled like onion. He was a decade older than Burgundy, broad in the shoulders, with the russet-and-white coat of a mountain breed. He'd been mining since he was a pup. His left ear was notched in three places from accidents Burgundy had asked about exactly once. Tobin hadn't answered, and Burgundy hadn't asked again.

"My ma says if you don't eat, she'll come down here herself."

"Your ma scares me."

"She should."

Burgundy laughed, a small dry thing, and unwrapped the bread. He ate it standing up, one paw still on the keyboard. Tobin leaned against the workbench and folded his arms and watched him eat the way you watch a candle in a draft.

"Tomorrow, then," Tobin said.

"Tomorrow."

"And if he says no?"

The hours-since-sleep counter ticked over to thirty-two.

"He won't," Burgundy said.

"And if he does?"

Burgundy swallowed the last of the bread. He wiped his paw on his coveralls. He looked across the basement at the tarp, at the bulk of the thing beneath it, and he thought of all the nights he had spent down here — every night for two years, every night since he had first taken the back of an industrial drill apart and put it together again, badly, and then again, less badly, and then again, until he understood why the engineers had done what they had done and why he could do it better.

"He won't," he said again. Softer this time. Tobin didn't press it.

He didn't sleep. He stood at the workbench until the great clock in the palace tower struck five, and then he washed his paws in cold water from the basin in the corner, and he changed his shirt, and he pulled the tarp off the prototype, and he wheeled it out toward the stairs.

It rolled on a single fat wheel. He had built it that way because he hadn't had enough good ball bearings for two. The wheel had been salvaged from a serving cart. The frame had been salvaged from a broken loader in the southern shaft. The processing core was the only part that was his — the only part he had built from nothing — and it sat in the chest of the machine where a heart might have gone, behind a panel of green glass that glowed when it had power.

He stood at the bottom of the stairs and looked up the spiral, three full turns of stone steps to the receiving hall above. The hum of the prototype filled the silence.

"All right," he said to it. "Don't make me look like a fool."

The green panel pulsed once, gently, as if it had heard him.

He went up.

## Chapter Two — The Court

The receiving hall of the palace was not beautiful. The King had spent his wealth on other things — vaults, mostly, somewhere below. The hall itself was tall and cold and made of grey stone the color of wet ash. There were no tapestries. There were no banners. At the far end, on a dais three steps high, sat the throne.

His father sat on it.

The King was a big puppy, broad through the chest, with a black-and-tan coat going silver at the muzzle. His eyes were the same color as the hall. When Burgundy had been very small he had thought his father was the largest thing in the world. He still thought so sometimes, in dreams.

His mother stood beside the throne. Mira's coat was the dust-grey of a desert breed, and she wore a gown the color of dried blood. She did not smile when she saw her son. She did not need to. Burgundy felt her watching him with the kind of attention that could pin a moth to a card.

"You asked to see me," the King said.

"Yes, sir."

"This is the council hour."

"I know, sir."

"Then I assume you have something worth the council's time."

Burgundy stepped forward. The prototype rolled with him. Three advisors stood at the foot of the dais — old puppies in old robes, the kind of puppies who had been advisors when Burgundy was born and would be advisors when Burgundy died. They watched without expression.

"Father," Burgundy said. "I want to show you something."

He gestured. The prototype rolled forward and parked itself in the open space at the foot of the dais and lit up. The panel on its chest pulsed green. It made a soft sound, almost like clearing its throat.

"It mines," Burgundy said. "Or it will, in the field. Twice as fast as a puppy with a pick. Maybe more, with proper tuning. I built it from scrap. The components cost less than a month's pay for a single miner. If we built a hundred —"

"Stop."

The King had not raised his voice. He did not need to.

"Father —"

"I said stop."

Burgundy stopped. The hall was very quiet. The advisors were looking at the floor. Mira was looking at Burgundy. The King was looking at the prototype as if it had crawled in on its own.

"What do you imagine you have built?" the King asked.

"A miner. A better one."

"And what do you imagine I will do with it?"

"Whatever you want. You're the King. You could —"

"I am the King." His father said it gently, almost patiently, the way one might explain a familiar fact to a slow child. "And do you understand what that means?"

"It means —"

"It means I rule. Not metals. Not machines. Puppies. I rule puppies. I do not need to mine the mines faster. I need to rule the puppies who mine them. Do you understand the difference?"

Burgundy did not answer. His throat was too tight.

The King stood. He was a tall puppy, and he came down the three steps slowly, his robe whispering on the stone, and he stopped in front of the prototype. He looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at his son.

"You think I haven't seen this before," he said. "You think you're the first puppy to bring a clever toy to a throne. There have been a hundred of you. A thousand. Every one of them believed he had discovered the thing that would change the world. Do you know what changes the world, Burgundy?"

"What."

"Will. Will, and patience, and the willingness to do unpleasant things to puppies who are not as clever as they think they are." The King's voice was almost kind. "You are my son. You will rule one day. That is a craft. It is not learned in a basement. It is learned at my side. And you have not been at my side. You have been hiding in the dark, playing with wire, while I have been ruling. What I have given you is the only thing in this world worth having. And you bring me *this*."

He kicked the prototype.

It was not a hard kick — the King was a careful puppy — but the prototype rocked back on its single wheel, and the green panel flickered, and one of the advisors made a soft sound that might have been a laugh and might have been a cough.

"Take it away," the King said.

"Father —"

"Take it away, Burgundy. Do not bring it back. Do not bring me anything like it again. The next time I see you in this hall I want to see you in council robes. Listening. Learning. Not in coveralls. Not with grease on your paws. Do you understand me?"

Burgundy looked down at the prototype. The green panel was still flickering. He thought, with sudden cold clarity, that if he reached down and put his paw on the panel he would feel it trying. The little machine was still trying. It did not know it had been kicked.

"Yes, sir," he said.

He pushed the prototype out of the hall himself. None of the guards moved to help.

In the corridor, when the great doors had closed behind him, he stopped, and he stood with his paw on the prototype for a long time, and he did not cry, because he had not cried since he was eight years old, and he did not start now.

Footsteps behind him.

He turned. Mira was there. She had followed without him hearing.

"Mother."

"Walk with me," she said.

They walked. The prototype rolled between them, humming. Mira did not look at it. She looked straight ahead, at nothing.

"Your father is not wrong about everything," she said.

"He's wrong about this."

"Yes. He is wrong about this."

They walked another twenty paces in silence. Then she stopped, and she turned to him, and she put one paw on his cheek. Her paw was cool and very steady.

"Burgundy," she said. "You are not the first clever puppy in this palace. But you may be the first one with the patience to use it. Take it underground. Try it where he cannot see. And when you have learned what it can really do — when you *really* know — come and tell me. Do not tell him. Come and tell me."

She lowered her paw.

"Go," she said.

He went.

## Chapter Three — The Shaft

The South Twelve shaft was the deepest in Puppy Town and the worst.

It had taken three lives in the last year. The seams ran badly through it, and the ventilation was old and ran loud, and the puppies who worked it earned a stipend two coppers more than the surface shafts — two coppers being, in the King's accounting, the price of a puppy's lung. Burgundy had been down it once when he was twelve. He remembered the smell. Wet rock and machine oil and something underneath it that was not quite rot, like the planet itself was old and going off.

He came down at midnight with the prototype on a maintenance cart. Tobin met him at the head of the shaft. Tobin was not alone. There was a small puppy with him, a young one — couldn't have been more than fourteen — with a coat the color of soot and one eye that was milkier than the other.

"This is Wren," Tobin said.

"Hi," said Wren.

"He's quiet," Tobin said. "He'll keep watch."

Burgundy nodded. He did not ask why Tobin had brought a fourteen-year-old. He had a guess. The mines did not stay open by hiring older puppies. The mines stayed open by hiring whoever was small enough to reach the seams.

They took the lift down. The cage rattled. The shaft was dark except for the work lights strung along the wall, and the work lights were strung at intervals that left long stretches of black between them. Wren held the prototype steady on the cart. Tobin did not speak. Burgundy watched the rock walls slide past and tried not to think about what would happen if his father found out.

His father would not kill him. That was the worst of it. His father would do something much smaller and much more permanent. He would take the basement.

The shaft floor at the bottom was wet. They wheeled the prototype to a face the foremen had marked for the next shift — a long pale seam of low-grade ore that ran along the right-hand wall, exhausted enough that no one would notice if the take came in a little high or a little low. Burgundy unhooked the cart. He opened the access panel on the prototype's flank. He plugged in his terminal — a portable, battered thing, screen the size of a hand, keys soldered on by his own paws — and he started it up.

The prototype hummed louder.

"You sure about this?" Tobin asked.

"No."

"Good. Be sure about the next part."

Burgundy keyed the start command.

The prototype rolled forward on its single wheel and met the rock face and engaged its drill. The drill was the part Burgundy was proudest of — variable-speed, with a feedback gauge that could read hardness in real time and adjust torque. The drill bit the rock. Chips flew. A neat hole began to open in the wall.

For about four minutes it looked like it was going to work.

Then the drill bit caught on a vein of harder stone and the prototype, which had been programmed to compensate, did not compensate fast enough, and the bit snapped, and the prototype kept going, grinding its broken stub against the wall like a puppy chewing a leg it had already lost.

Burgundy lunged forward and hit the kill switch.

The basement hummed itself down. The green panel pulsed, slower, slower, slower, and stopped.

The shaft was very quiet.

"Huh," Tobin said.

Burgundy stood with his paws on the housing of the prototype and stared at the broken drill bit. It had not even gone six inches into the rock. A miner with a pick would have done better. A miner with a pick would have done *much* better.

"Burgundy."

"Don't."

"Burgundy, it's all right —"

"I said don't."

Tobin shut up. Wren, very small in the dark behind them, did not say anything at all.

Burgundy stood there for a long time.

He thought about going upstairs and putting the prototype in the furnace. He thought about going upstairs and putting *himself* in the furnace. He thought about his father's face in the receiving hall and the small sound the advisor had made, the soft cough that had not been a cough, and he thought about Mira's paw on his cheek — *when you really know, come and tell me* — and he stood there with his paw on the broken machine and the rage of it rose in him until it was something almost beautiful, something almost like clarity.

He had been trying to design the right machine.

He had been doing it wrong.

The right machine could not be designed. The right machine had to *learn*.

He sat down on the wet floor of the shaft with his terminal in his lap and he began to type.

It took him until almost dawn.

He did not write a new program for the prototype. He wrote a small program that wrote programs for the prototype — a loop, a feedback loop, that would let the prototype try a thing and score the result and try the thing again, slightly differently, and score that result, and try again, ten times a second, a hundred thousand times an hour, two million times a day. The prototype would not know what it was doing. The prototype would only know whether the last thing it had done had worked better than the thing before it. And it would keep what worked, and it would throw out what didn't, and it would do this with no instruction from him at all.

He didn't know if it would work.

He swapped out the broken drill bit for a spare. He keyed the new program in. He patched the terminal into the prototype's processing core and left the terminal there, tucked behind the housing. He started the loop.

The green panel pulsed. The drill spun up. The prototype rolled forward and met the wall.

It mined badly. A child with a spoon could have mined better.

"Burgundy," Tobin said.

"Leave it."

"Leave it doing *that*?"

"Leave it. A week. Don't touch it. Don't let anyone touch it. If a foreman comes, tell him it's a survey unit from the surface."

"A week."

"A week."

He climbed back into the lift. Wren rode up with him. Tobin stayed at the bottom, watching the prototype scrape and stutter against the rock face like a puppy too dumb to give up.

At the top, in the cold dawn air, Wren spoke for the first time.

"What if it doesn't get better?"

Burgundy looked at him.

"Then I'll have to think of something else."

## Chapter Four — The Week

The week was longer than any week Burgundy had ever lived through.

He went to council. He put on the robes. He sat at his father's left hand and he kept his face empty while his father discussed tariffs and tribute and the production quotas of the northern shafts. He nodded at the right times. He said nothing. The advisors looked at him sometimes with a kind of small satisfaction, as if a difficult problem had finally been put away in its drawer.

On the second day, the tribute ships came in.

Burgundy watched them from the high windows of the council chamber — three of them, long and dark, settling into the field beyond the western wall with the slow precision of insects laying eggs. The ships were not Puppy Town ships. The ships were Nicholas's. They came once a season, and they left full, and when they left they took with them most of what the mines had pulled out of the planet that quarter. He had seen them all his life. He had stopped noticing them, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat, until something makes you notice it again.

He noticed them now.

His father stood beside him.

"He has been generous this quarter," the King said. "He has accepted the southern ore at grade two even though it is barely grade three. He is a friend to this house."

"Yes, sir."

"You will deal with him someday. You will need to be ready."

"Yes, sir."

His father looked at him sideways. There was something in his eye that was not warmth but was nearer to warmth than anything Burgundy had seen there in a long time.

"You're learning," the King said.

"Yes, sir."

His father clapped him on the shoulder — a small, hard, unfamiliar gesture — and walked away.

Burgundy stood at the window for a long time and watched Nicholas's ships sit on his father's field.

On the third day Mira found him in the library. She did not say anything to him about the prototype. She said something to him about a passage in a book of old governance — a passage about how a king's power was made of two things, the strength of his arm and the patience of those beneath it, and how the second was the larger of the two, and the more easily lost.

He understood her perfectly.

On the fourth day Tobin sent up a note through a kitchen puppy who owed him a favor.

*Still going,* the note read.

That was all.

On the fifth day there was a small accident in the north shaft. Three miners hurt, one badly. The King did not visit. The King did not send a representative. The King continued council as scheduled. Burgundy went to the infirmary alone that evening and sat with the badly hurt one — a puppy named Carrow whose left foreleg had been crushed under a beam — and he listened while Carrow tried to make a joke about how he wouldn't have to lift anything for a while and then began to cry in the embarrassed way that adult puppies cry when they don't think they should be crying.

Burgundy held his paw. He did not know what to say. He thought about the prototype in the dark of the South Twelve shaft, trying and failing and trying again, and he thought of how he might have built it differently — quieter, gentler, with sensors that could feel a beam beginning to fail before it failed — and he thought how many Carrows there had been in this town, in this town his father loved to rule.

On the sixth day his father took him hunting.

It was the first time in seven years.

They went out into the low hills beyond the palace with a brace of dogs — actual dogs, not puppies, the dumb four-legged kind kept for sport — and they walked all morning and shot nothing. The King talked the whole time. He talked about his own father, who had been a hard puppy, who had once broken a councillor's jaw at table for speaking out of turn. He talked about Nicholas, whom he had met three times in his life and feared more than he liked to admit. He talked, with a kind of distant pride, about Burgundy as a small pup — about a time when Burgundy had taken apart a clock and put it back together at the age of five, and the King had taken the clock to a watchmaker, and the watchmaker had said it was running better than when it had been new.

"You see," the King said. "I remember."

"Yes, sir."

"I have not forgotten what you are."

"No, sir."

"Don't make me forget."

The hunt ended at dusk. They walked back to the palace in silence. Burgundy felt his father's hand on his shoulder once, just before they reached the gates, and the weight of it was the weight of a stone.

That night Burgundy went down to the basement and did not sleep.

The seventh day was the day.

## Chapter Five — The Return

He went down at midnight again. Tobin met him at the head of the shaft. Wren was there too — Wren was there every night now, apparently, although Burgundy had not asked him to be — and a third puppy Burgundy did not know, a wiry female with a brindle coat and a dark patch over one eye.

"Sable," Tobin said by way of introduction. "She mines South Twelve. She knows where to keep her mouth."

Sable nodded once. She did not speak.

They took the lift down. The cage rattled. The shaft was dark. Burgundy could feel his own heart in his throat and he was glad of the dark because if Tobin had been able to see his face he would have known.

At the bottom they walked along the wet floor toward the face where Burgundy had left the prototype seven nights ago.

He heard it before he saw it.

It was not the sound he had left behind. He had left a stuttering, clumsy sound — a sound like a puppy with a spoon. This was a different sound. This was a long, even, almost musical hum, with a small regular click underneath it like the second hand of a clock. He stopped walking. Tobin stopped behind him.

"Is that —"

"Yes," Burgundy said. He started walking faster.

He came around the bend in the shaft and he saw it.

The prototype had moved. It was twelve meters past where he had left it. There was a long, neat tunnel cut into the rock face — a tunnel as wide as the machine itself, as clean as anything cut by a surveyor, the walls smooth and slightly warm — and the prototype was at the end of it, its drill turning in a slow steady rhythm, its body rocking forward and back in a small economical motion that Burgundy had never programmed.

Behind the prototype, in neat piles along the tunnel floor, was the ore.

Burgundy walked into the tunnel. He put his paw on the wall. It was warm and the dust did not stick to his paw. The cut was as smooth as glass.

He walked up beside the prototype. The green panel was pulsing — pulsing in a new rhythm, faster than before, in a pattern he had not written. He plugged his terminal in. He pulled up the logs.

The loop had run, in the seven days he'd been gone, a little over fourteen million times.

He read the parameters. The prototype had taught itself to vary the drill speed in response to mineral density. It had taught itself to rock its body forward at the moment of contact to apply pressure where it was needed. It had taught itself to *pause*, sometimes, for fractions of a second, when the drill was about to bind, and resume at a different angle. It had taught itself to sort ore from waste rock as it went — to spit the waste out behind it and stack the ore — using the chemical sensors he had built into its forward array for something else entirely. It had taught itself things he had not known were possible to teach.

He looked at the piles of ore.

He looked at the tunnel, twelve meters long and laser-smooth, cut in seven days by a machine that had not known what it was doing when he left it.

He sat down very slowly on the tunnel floor.

"Burgundy?" Tobin said. He was standing at the mouth of the tunnel. His silhouette against the work lights was small.

"Tobin."

"Is it — does it work?"

Burgundy looked at him. He felt something rising in his chest that was not joy, exactly, because it was too big to be joy. It was the feeling a puppy must get when he opens a door and finds a god behind it. It was not joy. It was the absence of any feeling small enough to be named.

"Yes," he said. "It works."

Tobin did not move. After a moment Wren stepped up beside him, and after another moment Sable, and the three of them stood at the mouth of the tunnel and looked down at Burgundy and at the machine that was still humming behind him, still mining, still teaching itself, still going.

"How much?" Tobin asked.

Burgundy did not answer for a while. He was doing math.

"By the numbers in the log," he said eventually, "this one machine, in seven days, has pulled more ore out of this seam than the South Twelve shift would pull in a month."

Nobody said anything.

"More than the whole shift," Burgundy said. "In a week. Alone. With no foreman. With no light. With no breaks."

"One machine," Tobin said.

"One machine."

"What if there were a hundred."

"A hundred would —" Burgundy stopped. He was doing the math again and it was getting away from him. "A hundred would mean we wouldn't need a shift at all."

"A thousand," Wren said quietly.

Burgundy looked at the small puppy with the milky eye. Wren had not said anything in two days. Now he was standing very straight, very still, looking at the prototype.

"A thousand," Wren said again, "and we wouldn't need *him*."

The tunnel was very quiet.

Burgundy stood up. He brushed the dust off his coveralls. He looked at the prototype, humming, working, teaching itself in the dark.

"No," he said. "We wouldn't."

He left the prototype where it was. He left it running. He told Tobin to bring him a list, by morning, of every miner in the South Twelve shaft who could be trusted to keep his mouth shut.

He went up the lift. He went back to the palace. He went past the council hall and the receiving hall and up the long spiral stairs to the wing where his mother slept.

He knocked.

She answered as if she had been waiting.

"Mother."

"Tell me," she said.

He told her.

## Chapter Six — The Conspiracy

What happened next happened in pieces, over months, in the dark, in the gaps between the things the King chose to notice.

The first piece was Mira.

The night Burgundy told her, she did not speak for a long time. She sat in the chair by her window with her paws folded and her face turned away. He waited. He had inherited her patience, even if she had not known he had.

When at last she turned to him, she said: "I have been waiting for you. I want you to know that I have been waiting for you for a long time."

She told him then about the vaults.

The King's wealth was real, but it was less real than the King believed. Most of it had been spent — on tribute, on his own indulgences, on the slow steady cost of being feared. What remained was concentrated in the lower vaults beneath the palace, and a portion of it, a not-small portion, was under Mira's seal. She had set aside an emergency fund decades ago, when Burgundy was still a pup, against a future she had not been able to name but had known was coming. The King had not asked about it. The King had not, in any meaningful sense, looked at her in fifteen years.

"How much?" Burgundy asked.

She told him.

He sat down on the edge of her bed.

"Spend it," she said. "All of it, if you have to. I have been keeping it for you."

The second piece was Tobin.

Tobin became, in the weeks that followed, something Burgundy had not seen him be before — a captain. He had been a miner all his life and he had never asked to be anything else, but he stepped into the work of organization the way a puppy steps into a coat that turns out to fit him. He knew every miner in South Twelve and most of the miners in the other shafts. He knew which ones drank and which ones gambled and which ones had family the King could threaten. He knew, more importantly, which ones could keep their mouths closed when their wives asked them why they were coming home so late.

He brought Burgundy a list of forty-three names. By the end of the month it was sixty. By the end of the second month it was over a hundred. The miners came down to the basement at night, in twos and threes, by the back stairs Mira had opened for them, and they watched Burgundy work, and they handed him scrap — bits of ore filched from the surface stacks, lengths of cable salvaged from broken loaders, processing chips pried out of equipment that had been written off as scrap. They did not ask for anything in return. They asked, sometimes, how it was going. Burgundy got into the habit of answering them honestly, and the habit grew on him until he could not easily answer anyone any other way.

The third piece was Sable.

Sable, it turned out, had a brother who flew.

Her brother flew a small fast ship out of a port two systems over, and he flew under three different names, and he ran cargo for whoever paid, and one of the puppies he occasionally ran cargo for was a captain named Brindle, who captained a longer faster ship out of nowhere in particular, and who occasionally, when the price was right and the cargo was interesting, made unscheduled stops in the orbital lanes above Puppy Town to deliver things that were not on the customs manifest.

Captain Brindle came down to the planet personally the third time.

He was a long, lean puppy with a coat that was actually brindle — striped dark and light along his flanks — and a missing tooth on the left side of his smile, and a way of standing that suggested he had never in his life been the second-most dangerous puppy in a room. He met Burgundy in the basement at three in the morning. He looked around at the bank of monitors and the stacks of components and the long tarp at the far wall that did not, anymore, cover the prototype but a row of them — six already, the first three improved versions of the original, the next three something new.

"Huh," he said.

"Hello," said Burgundy.

"You're the prince."

"I'm the prince."

"You're shorter than I expected."

"I'm seventeen."

"That'll do it." Captain Brindle walked the length of the basement. He stopped in front of the row of prototypes. He whistled, low. "And these are the things."

"These are the things."

"Sable says you need more material than you can buy."

"That's right."

"Sable says you'll pay above market."

"That's right."

"Sable says you're going to use these to kill your father."

The basement was very quiet. Burgundy felt the eyes of the prototypes on him, although the prototypes had no eyes — only the green panels, pulsing in their slow patient rhythms, learning whatever they were learning while their puppy maker held still.

"Yes," he said.

Captain Brindle's missing-tooth grin widened.

"Good," he said. "I hated your grandfather. I never met your father, but I assume I'd hate him too. When do you need the first shipment?"

The fourth piece was time.

Time passed. The mines kept producing — the official production, the production the King saw — and underneath it the prototypes proliferated. Burgundy built them in series of six now, then twelve, then twenty. The improvements compounded; each generation of prototype taught the next, and Burgundy could give them a new task and watch them learn it in hours instead of weeks. He built a second workshop in an abandoned ventilation chamber off the South Twelve shaft. He built a third in a sealed-off section of the eastern hills, where Captain Brindle's runners dropped cargo on moonless nights.

He stopped sleeping in his own bed. He slept in the basement, on a pallet, when he slept at all. His mother came down sometimes with tea and stood beside him while he worked. She did not always speak. She did not need to.

His father, in this same time, grew slower.

It happened the way it sometimes happens to puppies who have been afraid for a very long time without admitting that they were afraid. The King began to forget small things. He repeated himself in council. He spoke more often, in private, of his own father — of how his father had ruled, of how puppies in those days had been better, simpler, more grateful for what they were given. He did not notice that the production reports from South Twelve were running, that quarter, fractionally higher than they had ever run, and that the discrepancy was not in the ore but in the labor — that the foremen were reporting more output per worker than the workers themselves believed they were producing. He did not notice because he never asked his foremen anything. He asked his ministers, and his ministers told him what he wanted to hear.

He did not notice Burgundy at all.

The night the count reached two hundred, Tobin came down into the basement and stood at the foot of the stairs with his paws in his pockets and looked at the rows and rows of them — armored now, the new generation, the V1 series with the heavier chassis and the modular weapon mounts Burgundy had spent three months designing — and he did not say anything for a while.

"Two hundred," he said eventually.

"Two hundred."

"That's enough."

"It might be."

"It's enough, Burgundy."

Burgundy looked at the rows. The green panels pulsed in their patient rhythms. Two hundred of them, breathing in unison without knowing they were breathing in unison. He thought about the kick his father had given the prototype in the receiving hall — the small contemptuous tap of a boot — and he thought about Carrow with his leg crushed, and about the third advisor's small dry cough, and about Nicholas's ships sitting on his father's field, and about the look in his father's eye on the hunt when he had said *don't make me forget what you are*.

"All right," he said. "Two hundred."

He turned to Tobin.

"Tell them," he said. "Tomorrow night."

## Chapter Seven — The Crown

The palace was old, and it had been built in a time when puppies had imagined that wars came from outside, and so its walls faced outward, and its gates were thick, and its towers had narrow slit windows that pointed at the horizon.

It had not been built to defend itself from its own basement.

They came up at midnight through the spiral stair — Burgundy at the head, two hundred Battle Rico V1s rolling behind him on their reinforced tracks, the corridor filling with the low even hum of two hundred machines drawing in unison from the same patient power. Tobin was at his right. Sable was at his left. Behind the Ricos came the militia — sixty-eight miners with rifles Captain Brindle had brought down in the last shipment, and a hundred more with mining picks and pry bars and the wordless purposeful silence of puppies who had been quiet for a very long time.

The first guards at the head of the stair did not even raise their weapons. They saw the Ricos and they ran. Two of them dropped their rifles as they ran. Burgundy stepped over the rifles and kept going.

The alarm sounded somewhere above them, deep and slow, like a bell in a flooded church.

They came up into the lower colonnade and there the King's guard was waiting — fifty puppies in royal plate, with a pair of tracked tanks behind them, the same tanks the King had paraded through the streets at his last name-day. The captain of the guard saw Burgundy and lowered his pistol.

"Your Highness —"

"Stand aside," Burgundy said.

"Your Highness, I cannot —"

"Stand aside, Captain. I'll let you live."

The captain of the guard hesitated. Burgundy watched his face. He watched the small calculation happening in it — a puppy who had spent thirty years in the palace, who had a wife and three children in the city, who had known Burgundy as a small pup with his hand on the hilt of a wooden sword — and he watched the calculation come out on the side of the wife and the three children, and the captain lowered his pistol the rest of the way and stepped aside.

The tanks did not.

The tanks fired.

The first shell hit the lead Rico square in the chest. The Rico rocked back; the armor held; a chunk of marble blew out of the wall behind it, large enough to crush a puppy. The Rico's weapon mount swung up and the Rico fired back — a single shaped charge, designed by Burgundy in a workshop where he had not been sure he would ever use it — and the lead tank's turret came off in one clean piece and rolled across the marble floor like a coin.

The second tank fired twice and hit nothing.

The Ricos walked through them.

It was not, Burgundy thought afterward, exactly a battle. A battle requires that both sides be doing the same thing. This was something else. The Ricos were doing something new and the King's guard was doing something very old, and the new thing went through the old thing the way water goes through paper. He had built the Ricos to learn. They had been learning, in the months in the basement, in the workshops in the hills, in the long quiet nights of their slow patient pulsing. They had learned a great deal.

In four minutes they had cleared the lower colonnade.

In nine they had taken the upper hall.

In fifteen they were at the doors of the receiving chamber.

Burgundy stopped at the doors.

He turned to Tobin.

"Stay here," he said.

"Burgundy —"

"Stay here. Hold the corridor. I'll go alone."

"With —"

"With them," Burgundy said, and he gestured at the Ricos behind him. Two hundred of them, untouched, the green panels in their chests pulsing in the slow patient rhythm he had taught them and the new rhythms they had taught themselves. "With them. Not with you. I don't want you to see this."

Tobin looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded once. He stepped aside.

Burgundy pushed open the doors.

The receiving chamber was empty except for the throne and the puppy on it.

The advisors were gone. The guards were gone. Mira was gone — she had been moved hours earlier, by Burgundy's own arrangement, to a safe room in the lower vault. The torches along the wall were lit, and the great hall was very still, and at the far end on the dais sat the King.

He was not in his robe. He was in the simple dark tunic he wore in his private rooms. His crown was on his head. His paws were folded in his lap. He watched Burgundy come up the length of the hall with the same flat grey eyes Burgundy had seen all his life.

The Ricos followed Burgundy. They came in two long columns, a hundred to a side, and they spread out along the walls of the hall and they stopped, and they stood, and they pulsed. The hall was full of their soft low hum.

Burgundy stopped at the foot of the dais.

His father said: "You came up the back stairs."

"Yes, sir."

"That's not how it's done."

"It's how it was done tonight."

The King looked past him, at the rows of Ricos. He looked at them for a long time. His face did not change.

"How many."

"Two hundred."

"And you built them."

"I built them."

"From the toy."

"From the toy."

The King nodded slowly. Something in his face moved — not regret, exactly. Not even surprise. Something closer to recognition. He looked at his son the way a puppy looks at a stranger who turns out to have been standing in his own house for a long time.

"You should have shown me what they could do," the King said. "When you brought the first one. You should have made me understand."

"I tried, sir."

"You should have tried harder."

"Yes, sir."

The King stood. He came down the three steps slowly, his tunic whispering on the stone, and he stopped in front of his son. He took the crown off his own head.

It was not a beautiful crown. The Puppy King's crown was a simple gold band, scratched in places, with a single dull red stone set in the front. The King had worn it for thirty-one years. It had been worn by his father, and by his father before him, and by puppies whose names Burgundy had been made to memorize as a child and had since forgotten.

The King held the crown out.

"All right," he said. "Take it."

Burgundy reached out and took the crown.

It was lighter than he had expected.

He stood holding it for a long moment, his father in front of him, the rows of Ricos behind him, the great hall echoing very faintly with the hum of two hundred patient machines.

He thought, with a clarity that surprised him, of the night he had taken the prototype to the South Twelve shaft. Of the moment he had sat on the wet floor with his terminal in his lap and understood that the right machine could not be designed, that it had to learn. He thought of Carrow in the infirmary. He thought of his mother's paw on his cheek in the corridor. He thought of Tobin standing in the basement saying *two hundred. That's enough.*

He thought of his father's boot tapping against the prototype's green chest.

He looked at the puppy in front of him. The puppy was waiting. The puppy was, perhaps, even now, beginning to understand that what he had just done — handing the crown over — was supposed to be the end of the story. That there were rules. That one king surrendered to another and the surrendering one went into exile or into a quiet room with a window and a pension, and the new king let him, because there were always more enemies, and a defeated king was a useful object lesson, and a dead king was a martyr.

Burgundy understood the rules. He had been taught them. He had paid attention.

He set the crown on his own head.

He turned to the Ricos along the left wall.

He said, very quietly: "Kill him."

The Ricos along the left wall began to move.

His father did not run. His father did not speak. His father looked at his son, and there was, at the very end, something almost like respect in his face — the small surprised respect of a puppy who has been beaten at a game he thought he understood. Burgundy did not look away. He had decided already, in the long nights of the basement, that whatever else he became, he would not be a puppy who looked away.

It did not take long.

When it was over, he turned and walked the length of the hall and he pushed the doors open and he stepped out into the corridor where Tobin was waiting.

Tobin looked at his face. Tobin looked at the crown on his head. Tobin did not ask.

"Go and tell them," Burgundy said. His voice was steady. He had decided that too. "Tell the city. Tell the mines. Tell the foremen and the council and whoever else has not yet heard. Tell them there is a new king in Puppy Town. Tell them his name is Burgundy. Tell them the old king is dead."

"And the production," Tobin said. "What do I tell them about the production."

Burgundy thought about it.

"Tell them," he said, "that from tomorrow, every mine in Puppy Town builds Ricos. Tell them we are going to need a great many more."

He walked past Tobin. He walked down the corridor between the rows of his machines. The Ricos hummed as he passed. The green panels pulsed in their patient rhythm, learning, still learning, the small invisible loop running and running inside each of them, the loop he had written one night on a wet floor in a dark shaft, the loop that had begun all of this and would not, he understood now, ever stop.

He went down the spiral stair to the basement.

He sat down at his bench.

He pulled the keyboard toward him.

There was work to do.
